\chapter{Introduction and Motivation}\label{chap:intro}

Historically, the engineering of efficient continuous optimization algorithms required rigid mathematical derivations and extensive manual trial-and-error processes conducted by human experts over decades \cite{Nelder1965, Hansen2001}. This effort produced a vast array of general-purpose solvers, ranging from classical gradient-based methods (such as Gradient Descent and Quasi-Newton algorithms) to popular derivative-free heuristics, most notably the Nelder-Mead simplex algorithm \cite{Nelder1965} and the Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) \cite{Hansen2001}. However, due to the immense topological diversity of real-world fitness landscapes, no single general-purpose solver can achieve optimal performance across all problem instances without specialized adaptation. Custom-tailoring unique algorithmic logic or tuning parameters for every distinct application represents a severe engineering bottleneck, as manual design is heavily labor-intensive and unscalable \cite{Burke2013}.

This manual engineering bottleneck is further severely compounded when optimization algorithms are deployed in non-deterministic, noisy environments. Traditional ranking-based heuristics like CMA-ES frequently break down in noisy settings---chasing false statistical spikes and failing to converge without extensive, manually engineered statistical interventions \cite{Hansen2009Noisy}. Manually designing a robust mathematical algorithm to handle this uncertainty is notoriously difficult, as a fundamentally poor candidate solution may temporarily yield a high fitness score purely due to a favorable statistical anomaly, causing search rules to adjust parameters toward false gradients \cite{Jin2005}. This vulnerability is even more severe in classical gradient-based algorithms; because these systems rely entirely on computing exact analytical or finite-difference derivatives, even minor statistical noise completely corrupts gradient estimation vectors, leading to immediate algorithmic failure \cite{Sarafian2024}.

To automate the discovery of specialized search strategies and eliminate human design bottlenecks, techniques such as hyperparameter tuning, automated algorithm selection, and traditional Automated Algorithm Design (AAD) were developed long before the advent of deep learning. Recently, AAD in particular has experienced a major resurgence driven by Large Language Models (LLMs). Rather than relying on rigid, predefined search templates, modern AAD frameworks embed foundation models within structured, closed-loop evolutionary environments capable of autonomously writing, testing, and refining complete executable source code structures that rival or surpass traditional human-engineered baselines \cite{Romera2023}.

To operationalize this autonomous code-generation loop, this research leverages the Large Language Model Evolutionary Algorithm (LLaMEA) framework \cite{LLaMEA2024}. The system pipeline prompts the generative model to output fully functional Python optimization classes, executes the generated script within a secure validation sandbox against standard mathematical benchmarks, and iteratively feeds raw execution metrics and compiler tracebacks directly back into the LLM's context window to drive self-directed mutation and logical debugging. While existing literature proves that frameworks like LLaMEA deliver highly competitive optimization results when evaluated under complete determinism \cite{LLaMEA2024}, their capability to adapt code structures to unpredictable, non-deterministic conditions remains a major open problem. This thesis directly addresses this research gap by evaluating the performance and adaptive capacity of LLaMEA when deployed across continuous black-box optimization landscapes corrupted by persistent statistical noise, investigating whether closed-loop evolutionary feedback can drive language models to synthesize noise-resilient search strategies.

This thesis establishes a fundamental, benchmark-driven computer science evaluation to isolate the capabilities of Automated Algorithm Design under uncertainty. We emulate the chaotic behaviors of physical hardware sensors by constructing a controlled stochastic layer directly over the continuous Black-Box Optimization Benchmark (BBOB) suite \cite{Hansen2010}, which represents the most widely used benchmark suite for single-objective continuous optimization. By systematically corrupting synthetic math landscapes with a parameter-controlled statistical noise distribution, we establish a clean, repeatable evaluation loop to test the adaptive limits of the LLaMEA framework. This abstract configuration enables this research to study the core generative problem directly: assessing whether the evolutionary pressure of evaluating code within a stochastic environment can compel an LLM to autonomously discover and output specialized, programmatic Python optimization heuristics tailored to handle uncertainty.

The core contribution of this thesis is divided into three distinct methodological phases designed to establish a clear indicator of language model adaptability under severe uncertainty. First, we formulate a non-deterministic evaluation sandbox by building a dynamic wrapper layer capable of embedding controlled statistical profiles onto standard benchmark problems. Second, we evaluate the generative model's capacity to modify Python-based metaheuristic class structures when exposed exclusively to these corrupted landscapes over multiple evolutionary cycles. Finally, this research maps out a rigorous cross-environment robustness test, validating our noise-trained champion models directly against equivalents evolved under clean, deterministic criteria to isolate the structural logic an AI independently invents to protect its search path from deception.
