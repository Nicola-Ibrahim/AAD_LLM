\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

The development of continuous optimization algorithms has traditionally
combined mathematical analysis with empirical testing and refinement by
human experts. This work has produced methods with different search
mechanisms and requirements, including the derivative-free Nelder--Mead
simplex algorithm \cite{Nelder1965} and the Covariance Matrix Adaptation
Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) \cite{Hansen2001}. These methods are used
across diverse landscapes, including real-world optimization problems
and artificial benchmark functions. On the noiseless Black-Box
Optimization Benchmark (BBOB) testbed, relative algorithm performance
varies across benchmark functions, problem dimensions, target precisions,
and function evaluation budgets \cite{Hansen2010}. Selecting or adapting
a search method for a particular application therefore remains an
important part of optimization practice, motivating approaches that
automate heuristic selection and design \cite{Burke2013}.

This design problem becomes more difficult when objective evaluations
are affected by noise. Measurement errors and randomized simulations
can cause repeated evaluations of the same solution to return different
values. A candidate may consequently appear better than another even
when its underlying objective value is worse, affecting selection and
subsequent search decisions \cite{Jin2005}. Rank-based methods are also
subject to this problem: Hansen et al.\ developed an uncertainty-handling
method for CMA-ES that detects changes in candidate rankings through
re-evaluation and adjusts the search accordingly \cite{Hansen2009}.
For methods that estimate derivatives from function values, noise
introduces a further difficulty. Finite-difference estimates must balance
truncation error against noise amplification, making the differencing
step important for obtaining useful estimates \cite{MoreWild2012}.

Existing approaches to noisy optimization include repeated sampling,
larger populations, modified selection, and adaptive evaluation
strategies. These approaches allocate computational effort differently,
and their effectiveness depends on the algorithm and noise setting
\cite{Jin2005}. Noise handling is therefore not simply a matter of
choosing one fixed parameter configuration. It can also require changes
to how an algorithm gathers information, compares candidates, and
allocates its evaluation budget. The proposed research will investigate
whether such search logic can be generated and refined automatically.

Automated Algorithm Design (AAD) seeks to reduce the manual effort
required to develop effective algorithms. Earlier approaches include
genetic programming and the search over combinations of predefined
algorithmic components \cite{Burke2013,vanRijn2016}. Large Language
Models (LLMs) offer another way to explore algorithm implementations
by generating executable source code. FunSearch demonstrates how a
language model can be combined with an evaluator to iteratively improve
programs, yielding new cap-set constructions and online bin-packing
heuristics \cite{Romera2023}.

The proposed study will use the Large Language Model Evolutionary
Algorithm (LLaMEA) framework developed by Niki van Stein and Thomas
Bäck \cite{LLaMEA2024} to investigate code-based AAD for continuous
black-box optimization under noisy observations. The model will generate
Python optimization classes, which will be executed in an isolated
evaluation environment. Performance feedback and execution diagnostics
will guide further revisions. The original LLaMEA study evaluates
generated optimizers on noiseless BBOB functions. The proposed
experiments will examine generated optimizers under both noiseless and
noisy objective observations.

The study will introduce controlled statistical noise into selected
functions from the noiseless BBOB suite \cite{Hansen2009Noiseless}.
This will provide a repeatable setting in which to examine the
performance and behavior of LLaMEA-generated algorithms under different
noise conditions.

The proposed work will combine this evaluation environment with
algorithm generation and comparative benchmarking to investigate
whether exposure to noise during evolution improves the resulting
heuristics' performance and robustness.
