%==============================================================================
\chapter{Experiments}
\label{chap:experiments}
%==============================================================================

A low synthesis gap establishes performance on the selection instance.
Independent benchmarking examines whether selected optimizers remain
effective across instances, using search progress, target attainment,
and returned-solution accuracy.

The study first benchmarks champions under the function, dimension, and
noise conditions used during synthesis. Comparisons with CMA-ES,
Differential Evolution, and Particle Swarm Optimization provide reference
performance under the same objective-evaluation budgets. The noise-robustness design instead holds clean-synthesis champions
fixed while varying evaluation noise; the available evidence is discussed
separately.

%==============================================================================
\section{Synthesis Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_design}
%==============================================================================

The experiments vary the LLM, prompt mode and scaffold, BBOB function,
dimension, and observation noise level. The synthesis procedure and
full prompt definitions are given in
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.

Five functions from the noiseless BBOB suite are selected to examine
optimizers facing different search challenges
\cite{BBOB2009,Hansen2021COCO}. Sphere provides a separable unimodal
problem, while Rosenbrock introduces a curved valley and Discus introduces
strong ill-conditioning. Rastrigin and Gallagher 101 peaks extend the
assessment to multimodal landscapes with different degrees of global
structure. Table~\ref{tab:experiment_functions} summarizes the selected
functions. This subset supports comparisons across contrasting landscapes,
although it does not represent the full BBOB suite.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{BBOB functions selected for the experiments.}
    \label{tab:experiment_functions}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
        \hline
        Function & Name & Main characteristic \\
        \hline
        $f_1$ & Sphere & Separable and unimodal \\
        $f_8$ & Rosenbrock original & Non-separable curved valley \\
        $f_{11}$ & Discus & Unimodal and highly conditioned \\
        $f_{15}$ & Rastrigin & Multimodal with global structure \\
        $f_{21}$ & Gallagher 101 peaks & Multimodal with weak global structure \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Each function is considered at
\begin{equation}
    D \in \{2,3,5,10\}.
    \label{eq:experiment_dimensions}
\end{equation}

Synthesis uses BBOB instance 1 for each function and dimension.
Independent benchmarking uses instances 2--21, with one trial per
instance. Instance 1 is excluded from the benchmark summaries, so all
20 evaluation instances are distinct from the synthesis instance.
This assesses generalization to unseen instances of the same function
and dimension, not to unseen function families or dimensions.

The experiments use the heteroscedastic Gaussian noise model defined in
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, with
\begin{equation}
    \sigma \in \{0,\,0.05,\,0.1,\,0.2\}.
    \label{eq:experiment_noise_levels}
\end{equation}

The clean condition uses $\sigma=0$. For positive $\sigma$, the noise
standard deviation is $\sigma|f_{\mathrm{clean}}(x)-f^*|$, where $f^*$
is the optimum value of the function instance. These levels therefore
control noise relative to the clean optimality gap. Each stochastic
objective evaluation uses a new noise realization.

We distinguish synthesis noise, $\sigma_{\mathrm{synth}}$, from
benchmark noise, $\sigma_{\mathrm{eval}}$. 
Three locally hosted LLMs are included in the completed synthesis
experiments. Table~\ref{tab:llm_configurations} identifies the model
artifacts. The models were hosted through a separate Python LLM server,
which LLaMEA queried to obtain candidate programs.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{LLMs used during synthesis.}
    \label{tab:llm_configurations}
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X l}
        \hline
        Model & Quantization / format \\
        \hline
        Qwen2.5-Coder-14B-Instruct & Q4\_K\_M GGUF \\
        Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct & Q4\_K\_M GGUF \\
        DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite-Instruct & Q4\_K\_M GGUF \\
        \hline
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The synthesis experiments use two prompt modes, Implicit and Explicit,
each combined with Baseline, Thinking, Vectorization, or Guided. Explicit
mode identifies whether objective observations are clean or noisy.
Implicit mode does not explicitly identify the observation environment.

Each LLM covers 640 synthesis conditions: five functions, four dimensions,
two prompt modes, four scaffolds, and four noise levels
($5\times4\times2\times4\times4=640$). The three models therefore cover
1,920 model-specific conditions in total. Each synthesis run has a maximum
of 10 iterations and a configured budget of $10^6$ objective evaluations
per candidate. These counts describe distinct synthesis conditions;
multiple runs may contribute candidates to the same condition.
The case study in
Section~\ref{sec:generated_program_cases} identifies a historical
execution that exceeded it.

After synthesis, one champion is selected for each combination of model,
function, dimension, synthesis mode, prompt strategy, and synthesis noise
level. Where multiple completed synthesis runs cover the same condition,
their recorded candidates are considered together.

The champion is the candidate with the lowest recorded clean optimality
gap, equivalent to the highest synthesis fitness among successful
candidates. Equal gaps are resolved by preferring the candidate with fewer
recorded objective evaluations; exact ties retain the existing record
order.

Champion selection uses only synthesis measurements. The selected
algorithms are fixed before independent benchmarking, and benchmark
results do not influence their selection.

\section{Benchmark Setup}
\label{sec:independent_benchmark}
%==============================================================================

The selected champions are benchmarked against reference optimizers on
BBOB functions implemented in IOH \cite{deNobel2024,Hansen2021COCO}.
The distinction between synthesis and independent benchmarking is
described in Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_method}.

Each benchmark condition comprises 20 trials. Trial $r$, for
$r=1,\ldots,20$, uses BBOB instance $i_r=r+1$ and optimizer/noise
seed $42+i_r$, giving instances 2--21 and seeds 44--63. All optimizers
receive the same evaluation budget at a given dimension:
$B_{\mathrm{eval}}=10{,}000D$, corresponding to 20,000, 30,000,
50,000 and 100,000 evaluations for $D=2,3,5,10$, respectively.
The proportional scaling with dimension is consistent with the budget
scaling discussed in the COCO experimental procedure
\cite{Hansen2016Procedure}; the multiplier 10,000 is chosen for this
study. This is a dimension-proportional budget, not a fixed allowance
of $10^6$ evaluations per benchmark trial. The latter allowance applies
to synthesis candidates, as described in
Section~\ref{sec:experimental_design}.

Every objective evaluation counts toward the budget, including the
initial evaluation and repeated measurements of the same point.
The separate clean assessment of the returned solution does not count
toward this search budget. Once the budget is exhausted, further
requests return the last observed value without evaluating the
objective again. Optimizers may terminate early; unused evaluations
are not allocated to restarts.

Generated champions are subject to a 30-second timeout to prevent
stalled or non-terminating executions from blocking the benchmark.
Classical baselines have no equivalent timeout. The comparison therefore
uses equal objective-evaluation budgets, which measure search effort,
but different execution-time constraints.

The shared instance and seed schedule controls the trial settings,
although the optimizers consume random numbers differently.
Table~\ref{tab:reference_optimizers} lists the reference implementations
and their settings.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Reference optimizers used in independent benchmarking.}
    \label{tab:reference_optimizers}
    \scriptsize
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
    \begin{tabular}{p{0.16\textwidth}p{0.22\textwidth}p{0.53\textwidth}}
        \hline
        Optimizer & Implementation & Parameters / configuration \\
        \hline
        CMA-ES & \texttt{cma} 4.4.4 &
        Uniform initial point; $\sigma_0=(u-l)/6$; bounds enforced;
        default population/stopping rules. \\
        Differential Evolution & SciPy 1.18.0 &
        Population multiplier 15; \texttt{polish=False};
        $\mathrm{maxiter}=\max(1,\lfloor B_{\mathrm{eval}}/(15D)\rfloor)$;
        other parameters use library defaults. \\
        Particle Swarm & Repository NumPy implementation &
        $n=\min(50,\max(10,5D))$; $w=0.729$;
        $c_1=c_2=1.49445$; clipped positions;
        uniform initial positions and velocities scaled by 0.1. \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Performance is assessed using convergence curves, target-attainment
ECDFs, and returned-solution success rates, as defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:benchmark_performance_measures}.
A trial is successful when its returned solution meets
$\Delta f\leq\tau_{\mathrm{primary}}=10^{-8}$.
The secondary threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{secondary}}=10^{-2}$ is used
in additional reliability diagnostics. Confirmed execution failures
are assigned infinite returned-solution gaps and counted as
unsuccessful trials.

The native success-rate tables report each complete condition
separately, using its 20 terminal trial results. They present
percentages without confidence intervals to keep the configuration
grid readable; the reliability diagnostics calculate a 95\% Wilson
interval for each condition. Aggregate summaries, such as the
noise-robustness comparison, give each complete condition equal
weight, as defined in
Equation~\eqref{eq:benchmark_aggregate_success}. Their percentile
95\% confidence intervals use 1,000 bootstrap resamples of whole
conditions, keeping the 20 trials within each selected condition
together.

All native benchmark conditions are complete, their recorded code
hashes match the selected champions, and their records satisfy the
evaluation-budget and validity checks. These summaries include only
the 20 instance--seed pairs specified above. Historical results for
instance 1 remain available for archival diagnostics but do not
contribute to the current success rates or search profiles.

Model comparisons use the Baseline scaffold to keep the prompt
strategy fixed, while prompt-strategy comparisons are made within
each model. Comparisons between Implicit and Explicit modes require
matching functions, dimensions, scaffolds, and noise levels.

\section{Performance Results}
\label{sec:benchmark_results}
%==============================================================================

Results are presented first for Explicit synthesis and then for
Implicit synthesis, using selected convergence and ECDF plots
alongside condition-specific returned-solution success rates.
The native benchmark covers 1,920 conditions with 20 trials each,
giving 38,400 outcomes, including execution failures. Each prompt
mode contributes 960 conditions and 19,200 outcomes.

The paired plots show how search progresses and how quickly targets
are reached. Each function panel compares champions from the four
prompt strategies with CMA-ES, DE, and PSO. Convergence curves show
median best-queried clean gaps with interquartile bands, while ECDFs
show the proportion of trial--target pairs attained within a given
number of evaluations. These measures describe the points evaluated
during search. Reaching the display floor or attaining the full
adaptive target set does not establish that every trial returns a
solution meeting $\Delta f\leq10^{-8}$. Adaptive target sets also
vary by noise level, so ECDF heights across noise levels do not
represent the same absolute accuracy.
The plots carry the last recorded search values forward after
a run ends, up to the dimension-specific budget. The evaluation axes
therefore end at 20,000 for $D=2$ and 50,000 for $D=5$ in the
displayed profiles, rather than at $10^6$. Equal horizontal positions
represent equal numbers of objective evaluations; the maximum
allowance differs between dimensions.

The success-rate tables report the percentage of trials whose
returned solutions meet the primary threshold. Each block fixes
the model, synthesis mode, dimension, and noise level, with
functions in the rows and prompt strategies in the columns.
Each cell reports one champion's results over 20 trials, so one
successful trial corresponds to five percentage points. No cell
combines different functions, dimensions, or noise levels.

Native comparisons evaluate each champion under the function,
dimension, and noise level used during its synthesis.
Implicit champions are evaluated under their corresponding
synthesis conditions, having been generated without explicit
information about the observation environment. Each noise level
therefore uses its own synthesized champion; these comparisons
do not measure the noise sensitivity of a fixed algorithm.

The selected plots cover all three LLMs, with each model shown at
both $D=2$ and $D=5$ across the two prompt modes. Because model,
dimension, and noise vary between examples, the plots illustrate
search behavior rather than provide a controlled comparison of
models or modes. Appendix~\ref{app:benchmark_profiles} provides
the remaining matched profiles for all three models,
$D\in\{2,5\}$, and the selected noise levels.
Appendix~\ref{app:benchmark_success_rates} reports individual
success rates for all four experimental dimensions and all four
noise levels, including $\sigma=0.1$.

few targets on Sphere but nearly the full target set on Rosenbrock
and Gallagher.

    \widehat p_{c,10^{-8}}^{\mathrm{return}}

threshold or fail to return a valid solution; both remain in the
denominator. These percentages measure the returned solutions,
rather than the best points evaluated during search or attainment

\section{Contrasting Generated Algorithm Designs}
\label{sec:generated_program_cases}
%==============================================================================

The aggregate results describe performance, but do not show how the
generated programs make decisions. This section examines two selected
champions produced through LLaMEA: a genetic algorithm with repeated
observation averaging, and an evolution strategy with an instance-sensitive
selection rule. These are qualitative examples of generated designs and
implementation weaknesses, not a controlled performance comparison.
They differ in LLM, prompt condition, function, dimension, and noise level;
their success rates therefore cannot isolate the effect of any one
mechanism or establish an advantage of one model or prompt condition.
The programs implement established algorithm families rather than
demonstrate the invention of a new optimizer. Their complete, unchanged
source is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:generated_programs}.

\subsection{Genetic Search with Observation Averaging}
\label{subsec:generated_ga_case}

Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct generated
\texttt{ImprovedNoisyGeneticAlgorithm}, the Explicit Vectorization champion
for Gallagher 101 peaks ($f_{21}$), $D=2$, and $\sigma=0.2$
(experiment 1298, iteration 6). Unlike the PSO, DE, and CMA-ES reference
optimizers, this program uses a genetic-algorithm structure: a population
of 100 individuals, five-member tournament selection, coordinate-wise
arithmetic crossover, Gaussian mutation, and 10\% elitism. Crossover is
applied with probability 0.7; mutated coordinates are clipped to the bounds.
The Gaussian mutation perturbs candidate coordinates and is distinct from
the heteroscedastic observation noise supplied by the objective.

Its observation-aggregation mechanism is explicit. Each candidate is
evaluated five times, and selection uses the arithmetic mean:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=championprogram,firstnumber=70,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
    def evaluate_population(self, population, problem):
        fitnesses = []
        for candidate in population:
            evaluations = [problem(candidate) for _ in range(self.evaluations_per_candidate)]
            fitnesses.append(np.mean(evaluations))
        return np.array(fitnesses)
\end{lstlisting}
For a fixed point $x$, averaging five independent observations under the
specified Gaussian noise model preserves the clean objective as the
expected value and reduces the observation variance to one fifth of the
single-call variance. The standard deviation is reduced by a factor of
$\sqrt{5}$, but is not eliminated. Retained elites are also re-evaluated
when the next population is assessed.

This reduction has an evaluation cost. Each population assessment consumes
$100\times5=500$ objective calls. The 20,000-call benchmark allowance
therefore permits 40 population assessments, including initialization;
the resulting 4,000 candidate assessments need not involve distinct points
because elites may recur.
The internal counter includes all five calls per assessment of a candidate.
The unchanged champion returns finite solutions in all 20 held-out trials:
15 meet the primary threshold $\Delta f\leq10^{-8}$ (75\%), and 19 meet
the secondary threshold $\Delta f\leq10^{-2}$ (95\%). Every trial uses
20,000 actual objective evaluations. Its recorded synthesis gap is zero,
but that single selection measurement does not imply success on every
held-out instance.

The code also adjusts mutation probability using population diversity:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=championprogram,firstnumber=65,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
            diversity = np.mean(np.std(population, axis=0))
            self.mutation_rate = 0.1 + 0.3 * (diversity / (ub - lb)).mean()
\end{lstlisting}
This changes exploration according to the spread of coordinates; it does
not estimate observation-noise variance. Likewise, the replication count
remains fixed at five regardless of uncertainty or the remaining budget.
Averaging can stabilize comparisons, but allows fewer candidate assessments
than single-observation evaluation at the same call budget.

A further limitation is the historical incumbent. Lines 61--62 of
Listing~\ref{lst:generated_ga_complete} replace the stored best only when a
new averaged value is lower. Although elites receive new observations,
the stored best value is not revised upward when re-evaluation is less
favorable. An unusually low noisy average can therefore remain the
reference for later improvements. Averaging reduces observation variability
without removing this optimistic-selection effect. No paired ablation
disabling averaging was performed, so the observed success rates do not
quantify its causal contribution. The source demonstrates an implemented
noise-handling mechanism, not that the LLM understands noise or that the
mechanism is sufficient for general noise robustness.

\subsection{Evolutionary Search with an Offset-Sensitive Filter}
\label{subsec:generated_ses_case}

The contrasting example is \texttt{ImprovedSES}, generated by
DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite-Instruct and selected as the Implicit Baseline
champion for Sphere ($f_1$), $D=5$, and $\sigma=0.05$
(experiment 2139, iteration 9). Its recorded synthesis gap is approximately
$1.294\times10^{-8}$ on the synthesis instance, whose optimum is $79.48$.
The program selects parents, adds clipped Gaussian mutants, and reduces
mutation strength with its internal evaluation counter. After more than
ten history entries, a condition on recent population minima can halve
the mutation strength. This is a history-based search heuristic, not an
estimate of measurement uncertainty. Surviving points can be evaluated
again, but their repeated observations are not combined into an averaged
fitness; the incumbent is retained using individual noisy observations.

Its parent filter, in lines 32--33 of
Listing~\ref{lst:generated_ses_complete}, is:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=championprogram,firstnumber=32,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
good_candidates_index = fitnesses[idx] < self.best_y * 1.1
parents = parents[good_candidates_index]
\end{lstlisting}
Of the 20 held-out trials, eight return finite clean gaps and twelve
fail after 150 actual objective evaluations. The median returned-solution
gap is therefore infinite. All twelve failures have negative instance
optimum values, while all eight finite-return trials have positive ones.
None meets the primary threshold, and five meet the secondary threshold:
the finite-return rate is 40\%, with primary and secondary success rates
of 0\% and 25\%. These terminal outcomes differ from search progress;
even failed trials queried points with finite clean gaps before the
program failed to return.

The filter depends on the absolute objective offset. For noisy observation
$\widetilde f(x)$, stored best observation $s$, and common additive
constant $c$,
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde f(x)+c < 1.1(s+c)
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    \widetilde f(x)<1.1s+0.1c.
    \label{eq:ses_offset_sensitivity}
\end{equation}
Shifting both the objective and its optimum leaves clean gaps and the
heteroscedastic noise scale unchanged, but changes which parents survive.
For a negative incumbent, multiplication by 1.1 makes the cutoff more
negative rather than allowing a slightly worse candidate. For a positive
incumbent the cutoff is more permissive. Selection on a single synthesis
instance did not expose this dependence on objective offset.

At $D=5$, the first pass evaluates 50 initial points followed by 50
parents and mutants. The initial infinite incumbent permits all 25
selected parents to survive. After these 100 calls, the incumbent is
finite. The next pass evaluates the 50-point population; if every parent
is rejected, the new population is empty. Line 41 then calls
\texttt{np.argmin} on an empty array before returning the stored best
point. Each current failure consumes only $150/50{,}000=0.3\%$ of its
benchmark allowance, so it is not caused by budget exhaustion.

Bounded diagnostics on the historical trial schedule reproduced this
empty-population error in its eleven failed trials. Adding or subtracting
a constant offset, while preserving the early queried coordinates and
underlying noise realizations, respectively removed or reproduced the
early failure. These checks support the offset-based explanation but do
not establish convergence of the shifted runs. The historical measurements,
including a synthesis-instance trial excluded from the current summaries,
and the diagnostic details are retained in
Section~\ref{app:ses_diagnostics} of Appendix~\ref{app:generated_programs},
including Table~\ref{tab:ses_instance_diagnostics}.

A separate defect concerns evaluation accounting. Let $P=50$ be the nominal
population size, $n$ the current population size, and $m$ the new size.
The two evaluation batches consume $n+m$ calls, but lines 22 and 39
advance the internal counter by $P+m-n$, an error of $P-2n$.
For the first 100 calls, the counter advances by only 50; with a stable
ten-point population, 20 calls advance it by 50. This affects both the
mutation schedule and stopping condition. The archived synthesis iteration
records 1,999,268 evaluations against its $10^6$ allowance. Independent
benchmarking instead caps actual evaluations at 50,000 for this condition.
Its smaller budget also prevents interpreting differences between synthesis
and finite benchmark gaps as instance effects alone.

\subsection{Interpretation of the Code Comparison}

The genetic algorithm explicitly aggregates repeated measurements, whereas
\texttt{ImprovedSES} combines mutation adaptation with single-observation
selection and a fragile acceptance filter. The examples show that a
generated program can contain a recognizable noise-handling mechanism
without eliminating noisy-selection bias, and that a small synthesis gap
can coexist with basic execution and accounting defects. They illustrate
design choices and limitations in particular generated programs, not
representative frequencies across all champions or causal differences
between the LLMs. Reliable interpretation therefore requires examining
source behavior alongside held-out returned-solution accuracy and
execution failures.
