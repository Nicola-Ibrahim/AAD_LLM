%==============================================================================
\chapter{Experiments}
\label{chap:experiments}
%==============================================================================

A low clean optimality gap during synthesis shows how a candidate performs
on the instance used to select it. It does not establish whether the
selected optimizer remains effective across other instances or under
different observation noise. The experiments therefore assess generated
optimizers beyond their synthesis evaluations, examining search progress,
target attainment, and the accuracy of returned solutions.

The study first benchmarks champions under the function, dimension, and
noise conditions used during synthesis. Comparisons with CMA-ES,
Differential Evolution, and Particle Swarm Optimization provide reference
performance under the same objective-evaluation budgets. A separate
noise-robustness study evaluates champions synthesized under clean
observations while varying evaluation noise and keeping each selected
program fixed. This separates performance under matched synthesis and
evaluation conditions from sensitivity to a changed observation environment.

%==============================================================================
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_design}
%==============================================================================

The experiments vary the LLM, prompt mode and scaffold, BBOB function,
dimension, and observation noise level. The following subsections specify
these configurations and the selection of champions for benchmarking.
The synthesis procedure and full prompt definitions are given in
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Problems and Dimensions}
\label{subsec:experiment_problems}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Five functions from the noiseless BBOB suite are selected to examine
optimizers facing different search challenges
\cite{BBOB2009,Hansen2021COCO}. Sphere provides a separable unimodal
problem, while Rosenbrock introduces a curved valley and Discus introduces
strong ill-conditioning. Rastrigin and Gallagher 101 peaks extend the
assessment to multimodal landscapes with different degrees of global
structure. Table~\ref{tab:experiment_functions} summarizes the selected
functions. This subset supports comparisons across contrasting landscapes,
although it does not represent the full BBOB suite.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{BBOB functions selected for the experiments.}
    \label{tab:experiment_functions}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
        \hline
        Function & Name & Main characteristic \\
        \hline
        $f_1$  & Sphere & Separable and unimodal \\
        $f_8$  & Rosenbrock original & Non-separable curved valley \\
        $f_{11}$ & Discus & Unimodal and highly conditioned \\
        $f_{15}$ & Rastrigin & Multimodal with global structure \\
        $f_{21}$ & Gallagher 101 peaks & Multimodal with weak global structure \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Each function is considered at
\begin{equation}
    D \in \{2,3,5,10\}.
    \label{eq:experiment_dimensions}
\end{equation}

Synthesis uses BBOB instance 1 for each function and dimension.
Independent benchmarking uses instances 1--20, with one trial per
instance. Instance 1 is therefore shared with synthesis, while
instances 2--20 provide additional landscape realizations.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Noise Levels}
\label{subsec:experiment_noise}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The experiments use the heteroscedastic Gaussian noise model defined in
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, with
\begin{equation}
    \sigma \in \{0,\,0.05,\,0.1,\,0.2\}.
    \label{eq:experiment_noise_levels}
\end{equation}

The clean condition uses $\sigma=0$. For positive $\sigma$, the noise
standard deviation is $\sigma|f_{\mathrm{clean}}(x)-f^*|$, where $f^*$
is the optimum value of the function instance. These levels therefore
control noise relative to the clean optimality gap. Each stochastic
objective evaluation uses a new noise realization.

The noise model is applied to noiseless BBOB functions through IOH;
the experiments do not use the official COCO \texttt{bbob-noisy} suite.
We distinguish synthesis noise, $\sigma_{\mathrm{synth}}$, from
benchmark noise, $\sigma_{\mathrm{eval}}$. The native and noise-robustness evaluations are described in
Section~\ref{subsec:experiment_robustness_design}.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Synthesis Runs}
\label{subsec:synthesis_runs}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Each LLM covers 640 synthesis conditions: five functions, four dimensions,
two prompt modes, four scaffolds, and four noise levels
($5\times4\times2\times4\times4=640$). The three models therefore cover
1,920 model-specific conditions in total. Each synthesis run has a maximum
of 10 iterations and a configured budget of $10^6$ objective evaluations
per candidate. This is the intended allowance; the case study in
Section~\ref{subsec:generated_program_cases} identifies a historical
execution that exceeded it.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Champion Selection}
\label{subsec:champion_selection}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

After synthesis, one champion is selected for each combination of model,
function, dimension, prompt condition, and synthesis noise level.
Where multiple runs cover the same condition, their recorded candidates
are considered together.

The champion is the candidate with the lowest recorded clean optimality
gap, equivalent to the highest synthesis score. Candidates with equal
clean optimality gap are ranked by the number of objective evaluations used;
remaining ties retain the existing record order.

This selection yields 1,920 champions for independent benchmarking.

Champion selection uses only synthesis measurements. The selected
programs are fixed before independent benchmarking, and benchmark
results do not influence their selection.

\section{Independent Benchmarking}
\label{sec:independent_benchmark}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Benchmark Protocol}
\label{subsec:experiment_benchmark_protocol}

The independent benchmark evaluates the selected champions and reference
optimizers on BBOB functions implemented in IOH
\cite{deNobel2024,Hansen2021COCO}. The separation between synthesis and
benchmarking is defined in Section~\ref{sec:benchmark_method}.

The evaluation budget is $B_{\mathrm{eval}}=10{,}000D$, giving 20,000,
30,000, 50,000 and 100,000 objective evaluations for $D=2,3,5,10$,
respectively. Each condition has 20 configured trials, using instance $r$
and optimizer/noise seed $42+r$ for $r=1,\ldots,20$.
Dimension-proportional budgeting follows the COCO experimental procedure
\cite{Hansen2016Procedure}; the multiplier 10,000 is selected for this
study. Optimizers at the same dimension receive the same evaluation
allowance. The smaller benchmark budget assesses the selected programs
across instances under a more restricted search allowance than synthesis.

The budget counts actual objective evaluations, including repeated
measurements of the same point. The initial evaluation consumes budget;
the separate clean assessment of the returned solution does not.
After the budget is exhausted, further requests receive the last observed
value without a new objective evaluation. An optimizer may stop early,
and unused budget is not spent through restarts. Each of the 20 trials
uses a different instance.

Generated champions have a 30-second execution timeout. Classical
baselines do not have an equivalent timeout, so their execution
constraints differ. Objective evaluations are the primary measure of
search effort. Elapsed time is recorded separately but is not directly
comparable because its measurement boundaries differ between optimizers.
The optimizers share the instance and seed schedule, although their
search procedures consume random numbers differently.
Table~\ref{tab:reference_optimizers} lists the baseline settings.

\subsection{Performance Measures}
\label{subsec:experiment_performance_measures}

Convergence, target-attainment ECDFs, and success rates are defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:benchmark_performance_measures}. The following
settings specify their use in the experiments.

Convergence figures show the median best clean optimality gap and its
interquartile range. They use a 300-point logarithmic evaluation grid
from 1 to $10^6$. The final attained value is carried forward after each
trial ends. Values beyond $B_{\mathrm{eval}}$ represent the same final
outcome and do not indicate further search or new target attainment.

The exploratory ECDFs use 51 logarithmically spaced target gaps derived
from observed positive gaps separately for each noise level. Let
$\tau_{\min,\sigma}$ and $\tau_{\max,\sigma}$ be the positive endpoints
selected for a plotted comparison. The target grid is
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{k,\sigma}
    =10^{\log_{10}\tau_{\min,\sigma}
    +\frac{k}{50}\left(\log_{10}\tau_{\max,\sigma}
    -\log_{10}\tau_{\min,\sigma}\right)},
    \qquad k=0,\ldots,50.
    \label{eq:benchmark_adaptive_targets}
\end{equation}
The targets are shared by the curves within a comparison. Their endpoints
are determined by the observed gaps rather than a fixed COCO target
schedule. ECDF heights across different target sets therefore do not
measure attainment of a common absolute accuracy level.

The primary success threshold is $\tau_{\mathrm{primary}}=10^{-8}$.
The model and prompt summary tables and the noise-robustness figure
assess the clean gap of the returned solution at this threshold.
These are returned-solution success rates, rather than the search-success
measure used for attainment during optimization. The tables include
complete conditions with 20 terminal trial results and weight conditions
equally, as defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:benchmark_aggregate_success}.
The secondary threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{secondary}}=10^{-2}$ is used
in additional reliability diagnostics.

\subsection{Uncertainty and Comparisons}
\label{subsec:experiment_uncertainty}

Per-condition returned-solution success rates use 95\% Wilson intervals.
Aggregate intervals use 1,000 condition-cluster bootstrap resamples,
seed 20260923, and percentile 95\% limits. Each resample selects whole
conditions, preserving their 20 trials together.

Incomplete conditions and conditions whose evaluated program does not
match the selected champion are excluded. Confirmed execution failures
remain in the assessment, with infinite returned-solution gaps and zero
successes. Timeout and runtime failures cannot be distinguished in the
available terminal results.

Model comparisons use the Baseline scaffold; prompt-strategy comparisons
are made within each model. Comparisons of Implicit and Explicit modes
require matching functions, dimensions, scaffolds, and noise levels.
Where groups contain different sets of complete conditions, differences
in aggregate success rates can also reflect their coverage. Restricting
comparisons to common conditions would remove that source of variation.

The chapter reports descriptive comparisons and bootstrap intervals.
Numerical hypothesis-test results are unavailable, so no statistical
significance is claimed. The recorded IOH and LLaMEA versions are 0.3.22
and 1.2.0, respectively. These and the library versions in
Table~\ref{tab:reference_optimizers} describe the recorded installations;
versions were not retained for every historical run.

\subsection{Noise Robustness}
\label{subsec:experiment_robustness_design}

Native benchmarking evaluates each champion on the function, dimension,
and noise level used during its synthesis, across instances 1--20.
The noise-robustness study instead evaluates an unchanged Explicit-Clean
champion under $\sigma_{\mathrm{eval}}\in\{0,0.05,0.1,0.2\}$ while
keeping its function and dimension fixed. No adaptation or champion
reselection occurs between noise levels. This design examines sensitivity
to evaluation noise separately from the synthesis environment.
Cross-function and cross-dimension transfer are outside the scope of the
reported experiments.

\section{Benchmark Results}
\label{sec:benchmark_results}
%==============================================================================

All 1,920 native benchmark conditions have 20 terminal trial results.
Eleven conditions are excluded because the evaluated code hash does not
match the currently selected champion for that condition. The descriptive
summaries therefore include 1,909 conditions, covering 38,180 trials.

The model and prompt tables report equally weighted returned-solution
success rates across eligible conditions. Convergence and ECDF figures
show each function separately. Raw gaps are not averaged across functions
because their scales differ.

In the native comparisons, each noise level uses its own synthesized
champion. These results describe the combined synthesis and evaluation
conditions. The later noise-robustness comparison holds the champion
fixed to examine changes in evaluation noise.

\subsection{Generated-Program Case Studies}
\label{subsec:generated_program_cases}

Aggregate success rates do not show how a generated program makes search
decisions or why a particular instance fails. The following two selected
champions illustrate distinct behaviors; they have different benchmark
conditions and are not a controlled head-to-head comparison.
The complete selected programs
are reproduced with line numbers in Appendix~\ref{app:generated_programs};
the excerpts below identify the decisions examined in this section.

\subsubsection{Successful Clean Search}

A DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite-Instruct Explicit-Clean Baseline champion for $f_1$ at $D=2$ is a generated
particle-swarm optimizer (experiment 2063, iteration 2). It maintains
personal-best positions and updates them when a new observation improves
the corresponding value:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=championprogram,firstnumber=41,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
improvement_mask = new_values < personal_best_values
personal_best_positions[improvement_mask] = particles[improvement_mask].copy()
personal_best_values[improvement_mask] = new_values[improvement_mask]
\end{lstlisting}
Its 20 benchmark instances all meet the returned-solution target
$\Delta f\leq10^{-8}$ within the $D=2$ budget of 20,000 evaluations.
This establishes success for this specified condition, not superiority
across the whole BBOB subset. The comparison in line 41 of
Listing~\ref{lst:generated_pso_complete} accepts an improvement by comparing
two objective values directly. Adding the same constant to both values
preserves their ordering. Similarly, the global incumbent is selected by
an \texttt{argmin}, which is unchanged by a common additive offset. This
avoids the sign-sensitive parent-selection rule found in the next example.
It does not establish that every implementation detail is ideal: complete
particle batches can make the optimizer request additional calls near the
budget boundary, where the benchmark wrapper enforces the actual evaluation
limit. The evidence here concerns its observed returned-solution performance
under the stated clean, two-dimensional condition.

\subsubsection{Offset-Sensitive Selection}

In contrast, the DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite-Instruct Implicit Baseline champion for noisy $f_1$ at
$D=5$ and $\sigma=0.05$ is \texttt{ImprovedSES} (experiment 2139,
iteration 9). Its recorded synthesis gap is approximately
$1.294\times10^{-8}$ on instance 1, whose optimum is $79.48$.
Selection on this instance therefore identified a near-optimal candidate
without testing whether its selection rule remained valid for other
objective offsets. Its parent filter, in lines 32--33 of
Listing~\ref{lst:generated_ses_complete}, includes the following lines:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=championprogram,firstnumber=32,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
good_candidates_index = fitnesses[idx] < self.best_y * 1.1
parents = parents[good_candidates_index]
\end{lstlisting}
Of its 20 benchmark trials, nine return finite clean optimality gaps and eleven
fail after 150 actual objective evaluations; the median returned-solution gap
is therefore infinite. All eleven failed trials have negative instance
optimum values, whereas the nine finite-return trials have positive
optimum values. Finite return is distinct from solving the problem:
none of the 20 trials meets the primary $10^{-8}$ target, and six meet
the secondary $10^{-2}$ target. The finite-return rate is $45\%$, while
the primary and secondary success rates are $0\%$ and $30\%$.
Table~\ref{tab:ses_instance_diagnostics} contrasts representative recorded
trials. Failed trials still have finite best-queried clean gaps in the search
history because the program evaluated points before failing to return.

The rule is sensitive to the absolute objective offset. If $s$ is the
stored best observation, adding a constant $c$ to the objective changes
the acceptance condition as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde f(x)+c < 1.1(s+c)
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    \widetilde f(x)<1.1s+0.1c.
    \label{eq:ses_offset_sensitivity}
\end{equation}
The same shift leaves clean objective gaps and the heteroscedastic noise
scale unchanged when $f^*$ is shifted with the objective. Nevertheless,
it changes which parents this program accepts. In particular, multiplying
a negative incumbent by $1.1$ makes the cutoff more negative, rather than
allowing a candidate to be slightly worse than that incumbent.
For positive incumbents the cutoff is more permissive, and its allowance
depends on the objective offset as well as the quality of the solution.
Thus a change in instance optimum is a valid benchmark condition that
exposes a weakness in the program's selection rule.

The full source explains the observed 150-evaluation failure. At $D=5$,
the initial population contains 50 points. The first pass evaluates those
50 points and then evaluates 50 parents and mutants. The initial
infinite incumbent allows all 25 selected parents to survive. After
100 objective calls, the program has a finite incumbent. The next pass
re-evaluates the 50-point population; if the parent filter now rejects
every candidate, the new population is empty. Line 41 then calls
\texttt{np.argmin} on an empty array before reaching the return statement,
despite the earlier stored best point.

An additional diagnostic executed the unchanged source with the recorded
instance and seed schedule, stopping surviving searches after at most 200
objective calls. It reproduced the empty-population \texttt{ValueError}
at 150 calls on all eleven previously failed instances. For instance 3,
the stored best observation after the first pass was approximately
$-241.626$, giving a cutoff of $-265.789$ and zero surviving parents.
For instance 1, the corresponding observation was $87.588$, its cutoff
was $96.347$, and five parents survived. The archived terminal records
do not contain these exception details; these local-state values and the
exception were obtained by the additional diagnostic, rather than inferred
from the median alone.

To isolate the role of the offset, the same diagnostic added $326.59$ to
the observations on instance 3, retaining the initial population and
optimizer/noise seeds. The first 150 queried coordinates and underlying
noise observations were identical; only the returned scalar offset was
changed before selection. Its shifted optimum became $79.48$, and the search continued
to the 200-call diagnostic limit. Conversely, subtracting $326.59$ from
instance 1 made its optimum $-247.11$ and reproduced the empty-population
failure at 150 calls. These bounded checks identify offset sensitivity in
the early selection logic; they do not establish final convergence of the
shifted runs or replace the archived benchmark measurements.

\subsubsection{Budget Counting}

A second weakness appears in lines 22 and 39 of
Listing~\ref{lst:generated_ses_complete}. If $P=50$ is the fixed nominal
population size, $n$ the current population size, and $m$ the new population
size, the two batches consume $n+m$ objective calls. The internal counter
instead advances by $P+m-n$, differing by $P-2n$. It undercounts when
$n=P$ and can overcount after the population shrinks. For example, the
first 100 actual calls advance the counter by only 50; with a stable
ten-point population, 20 calls advance it by 50. The mutation schedule and
stopping condition depend on this incorrect counter, so the defect can
also alter search behavior in trials that return a finite solution.
The archived synthesis iteration records 1,999,268 objective evaluations
against its configured allowance of $10^6$, exposing historical budget
noncompliance that the low recorded synthesis gap alone does not reveal.
Independent benchmarking caps actual IOH evaluations at 50,000 for this
condition; its counts should therefore be used instead of the program's
counter when assessing effort. The much smaller independent search allowance
also means that the synthesis gap and the finite benchmark gaps cannot be
interpreted as a contrast of instance effects alone.

The eleven early failures consume only $0.3\%$ of the benchmark budget.
Their cause is the reproduced empty-population error, rather than budget
exhaustion. Observation noise can change candidate ordering, but the
offset-dependent comparison and absence of an empty-population guard
explain this particular failure pattern. More evaluations do not repair
that rule. Conversely, the nine finite-return trials still require
separate quality assessment: their completion does not imply that the
optimum was found. This distinction motivates reporting finite returns,
target successes, and failures together with the terminal median.
