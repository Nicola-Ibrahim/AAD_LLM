%==============================================================================
\chapter{Experiments}
\label{chap:experiments}
%==============================================================================

This chapter evaluates the proposed LLM-based algorithm synthesis approach on
a selected set of continuous black-box optimization problems. The experiments
are designed to examine two related aspects of the synthesis process. First,
the experiments investigate whether providing the language model with
information about the optimization environment affects the algorithms that it
generates. Second, they examine whether the resulting algorithms remain
effective when they are evaluated under conditions that differ from those
encountered during synthesis.

The experimental evaluation is divided into three main parts. The first part
considers algorithms synthesized with explicit information about the
optimization environment. The second part removes this information and
evaluates algorithms generated under the implicit setting. The third part
takes selected synthesized algorithms out of their original synthesis
conditions and evaluates them on unseen benchmark functions, dimensions, or
fitness environments. The results from these experiments are then brought
together to identify the main findings and discuss their implications for
LLM-based algorithm synthesis.


%==============================================================================
\section{Experimental Design and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:experimental_design}
%==============================================================================

The experiments are based on continuous black-box optimization problems.
This setting is appropriate for the study because the optimizer has access to
fitness evaluations but does not have access to the analytical form of the
objective function or its derivatives. The generated algorithms must
therefore determine their search behaviour from the fitness values returned
by the objective.

The experimental design distinguishes between the environment in which an
algorithm is synthesized and the environment in which its final performance
is measured. During synthesis, candidate algorithms are generated and
evaluated as part of the evolutionary process described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}. Once an algorithm has been retained from this
process, its source code is fixed. It is then evaluated independently using a
common benchmark protocol. This separation is important because the
performance reported in this chapter should describe the behaviour of the
synthesized algorithm rather than its ability to continue adapting during
benchmark evaluation.


%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Benchmark Functions
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Five functions from the BBOB benchmark suite are used in the experiments:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Sphere (function 1),
    \item Rosenbrock (function 8),
    \item Discus (function 11),
    \item Rastrigin (function 15), and
    \item Gallagher 101 (function 21).
\end{itemize}

The functions were selected to expose the synthesized algorithms to
substantially different optimization characteristics rather than evaluating
them only on one type of objective. In particular, the selection covers
separable and non-separable problems, different conditioning properties, and
different levels of multimodality.

The Sphere function serves as the simplest reference problem in the
benchmark set. It is smooth, unimodal, and separable, which means that the
variables can be optimized independently. Its relatively simple structure
makes it useful for observing whether a synthesized algorithm can identify
and exploit a straightforward search problem.

The Rosenbrock function introduces a substantially different structure. It is
non-separable and contains a curved valley leading towards the optimum.
Although the function has a single global optimum, finding it efficiently
requires the optimizer to follow the geometry of the valley. It therefore
provides a test of whether an algorithm that performs well on a simple
separable objective can also handle a more coupled search problem.

The Discus function represents a strongly conditioned problem with an
asymmetric structure. One direction has a substantially different scale from
the remaining directions. This makes the function useful for examining the
behaviour of synthesized algorithms when the objective geometry is poorly
conditioned.

Rastrigin introduces a different source of difficulty through strong
multimodality. The objective contains many regularly distributed local
optima, making premature convergence and insufficient exploration important
concerns. Its inclusion allows the experiments to examine whether the
generated algorithms can maintain exploration when a simple local
improvement strategy is not sufficient to locate the global optimum.

Finally, Gallagher 101 provides a more irregular multimodal problem. The
function contains many Gaussian peaks with different characteristics and is
therefore less regular than Rastrigin. Its inclusion extends the benchmark
set beyond smooth unimodal and regularly multimodal functions and provides a
test of the synthesized algorithms on a more deceptive objective structure.

The resulting benchmark set covers the following progression:

continuous black-box optimization problems. Instead, it provides a compact
set of qualitatively different objective structures that can be used to
test whether the behaviour of synthesized algorithms changes with the
difficulty and structure of the underlying function.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Deterministic and Stochastic Fitness
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The experiments consider both deterministic and stochastic fitness
evaluations. Under deterministic conditions, an objective query returns the
clean objective value

\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{clean}}(x).
\end{equation}

Under stochastic conditions, the optimizer instead receives a perturbed
fitness value. The stochastic objective follows the heteroscedastic Gaussian
model introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}:

\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{noise}}(x)
    =
    f_{\mathrm{clean}}(x) + z,
    \qquad
    z \sim
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
        0,
        \left(
            \sigma
            \left|
                f_{\mathrm{clean}}(x)-f^*
            \right|
        \right)^2
    \right).
\label{eq:experimental_noise_model}
\end{equation}

The noise magnitude therefore depends on the distance of the current
fitness from the optimum. Far from the optimum, the stochastic variation can
be larger, while it decreases as the clean objective approaches the optimum.

The experiments use the following noise levels:

\begin{equation}
    \sigma \in
    \{\text{[TODO: exact noise levels]}\}.
\label{eq:experimental_noise_levels}
\end{equation}

For a fixed benchmark function, dimension, and noise level, the same
objective model is used throughout the corresponding experiment. Individual
objective queries receive independent noise realizations. Consequently, the
optimizer cannot rely on a fixed perturbation associated with a particular
point in the search space.

The distinction between the fitness observed by the optimizer and the
fitness used to assess the final solution is important throughout the
experiments. The synthesized optimizer operates on the noisy observations
when stochastic fitness is enabled. The evaluation of its solution quality,
however, uses the clean objective. The clean optimality gap is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \Delta f(x)
    =
    \left|
        f_{\mathrm{clean}}(x)-f^*
    \right|.
\label{eq:experimental_optimality_gap}
\end{equation}

This measure is used consistently when comparing algorithms across
deterministic and stochastic conditions.

\section{Cross-Evaluation and Generalization}
\label{sec:cross_evaluation}
%==============================================================================

The previous experiments measure the performance of synthesized algorithms
under the benchmark conditions considered during synthesis. The next
experiment changes this relationship. Instead of evaluating an algorithm
only in the environment from which it was generated, selected synthesized
algorithms are transferred to conditions that were not encountered during
synthesis.

This experiment is important because good performance on the synthesis
conditions alone does not show whether the generated search strategy captures
behaviour that can be reused in another optimization problem. The
cross-evaluation therefore treats the synthesized algorithm as a fixed
artifact and changes the conditions under which it is evaluated.

Three forms of transfer are considered. The first changes the problem
dimension while keeping the benchmark function unchanged. The second changes
the benchmark function while keeping the dimension fixed. Where included in
the experimental matrix, the third changes both the benchmark function and
the dimension.

results therefore provide a consistent basis for comparing algorithms
generated under different synthesis settings.

algorithms under stochastic fitness, and their transfer to conditions that

research questions in the following discussion.

when relevant information about the optimization environment is available.

algorithms behave when that information is removed.

retention of algorithmic behaviour under changed conditions. The distinction
between dimension transfer, function transfer, and environment transfer is
important here because each represents a different type of change between
synthesis and evaluation.

The conclusions drawn from these experiments remain specific to the
benchmark functions, problem dimensions, stochastic objective model,
language-model configurations, prompts, and synthesis settings used in this
study. The results therefore do not imply that the same behaviour will hold
for arbitrary continuous black-box optimization problems or for other
language models.

language model on five BBOB benchmark functions with different optimization

Discus, Rastrigin, and Gallagher 101.

information about the optimization environment and then algorithms generated
without this information. Both groups were evaluated under deterministic and
stochastic fitness conditions using the same independent benchmark protocol.

The cross-evaluation experiments subsequently transferred synthesized
algorithms to benchmark functions, dimensions, and fitness conditions that
differed from those used during synthesis. The resulting comparisons
provided evidence about the dependence of synthesized search behaviour on
