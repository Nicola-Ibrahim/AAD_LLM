%==============================================================================
\chapter{Experiments}
\label{chap:experiments}
%==============================================================================

Three locally hosted LLMs were used to generate optimizers for five BBOB
functions under clean and stochastic observations. The synthesis experiments
vary the model, prompt condition, problem dimension, and noise level. After
synthesis, selected champions are benchmarked across 20 BBOB instances and
compared with CMA-ES, Differential Evolution, and Particle Swarm Optimization.
A separate noise-robustness evaluation tests clean-origin champions under
changed observation noise while keeping their code fixed.

%==============================================================================
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_design}
%==============================================================================

The synthesis procedure and prompt specifications are defined in
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}. The following settings determine the
conditions used in the experiments.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Problems and Dimensions}
\label{subsec:experiment_problems}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The experiments use five functions from the noiseless BBOB suite
\cite{BBOB2009,Hansen2021COCO}. These functions cover separable,
ill-conditioned, and multimodal problems, providing different search
challenges within a manageable subset of the suite.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{BBOB functions selected for the experiments.}
    \label{tab:experiment_functions}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
        \hline
        Function & Name & Main characteristic \\
        \hline
        $f_1$  & Sphere & Separable and unimodal \\
        $f_8$  & Rosenbrock original & Non-separable curved valley \\
        $f_{11}$ & Discus & Unimodal and highly conditioned \\
        $f_{15}$ & Rastrigin & Multimodal with global structure \\
        $f_{21}$ & Gallagher 101 peaks & Multimodal with weak global structure \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Each function is considered at
\begin{equation}
    D \in \{2,3,5,10\}.
    \label{eq:experiment_dimensions}
\end{equation}

Synthesis uses BBOB instance 1 for each function and dimension.
Independent benchmarking uses instances 1--20, with one trial per
instance. Instance 1 is therefore shared with synthesis, while
instances 2--20 provide additional landscape realizations.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Noise Levels}
\label{subsec:experiment_noise}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The experiments use the heteroscedastic Gaussian noise model defined in
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, with
\begin{equation}
    \sigma \in \{0,\,0.05,\,0.1,\,0.2\}.
    \label{eq:experiment_noise_levels}
\end{equation}

The clean condition uses $\sigma=0$. For positive $\sigma$, the noise
standard deviation is $\sigma|f_{\mathrm{clean}}(x)-f^*|$, where $f^*$
is the optimum value of the function instance. These levels therefore
control noise relative to the clean optimality gap. Each stochastic
objective evaluation uses a new noise realization.

The noise model is applied to noiseless BBOB functions through IOH;
the experiments do not use the official COCO \texttt{bbob-noisy} suite.
We distinguish synthesis noise, $\sigma_{\mathrm{synth}}$, from
benchmark noise, $\sigma_{\mathrm{eval}}$. Their evaluation mappings
are listed in Table~\ref{tab:cross_evaluation_matrix}.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Champion Selection}
\label{subsec:champion_selection}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

After synthesis, one champion is selected for each combination of model,
function, dimension, prompt condition, and synthesis noise level.
Where multiple runs cover the same condition, their recorded candidates
are considered together.

The champion is the candidate with the lowest recorded clean optimality
gap, equivalent to the highest synthesis score. Candidates with equal
clean error are ranked by the number of objective evaluations used;
remaining ties retain the existing record order.

The three completed models contribute 640 champions each, giving
1,920 champions for independent benchmarking. Multiple synthesis runs
covering the same condition therefore contribute one selected algorithm.

Champion selection uses only synthesis measurements. The selected
programs are fixed before independent benchmarking, and benchmark
results do not influence their selection.

\section{Independent Benchmarking}
\label{sec:independent_benchmark}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Benchmark Protocol}
\label{subsec:experiment_benchmark_protocol}

The independent benchmark evaluates the selected champions and reference
optimizers on the BBOB functions implemented in IOH
\cite{deNobel2024,Hansen2021COCO}. Each optimizer is evaluated under the
configured objective-evaluation budget, problem instances, and noise
conditions. Final solution quality, convergence, and target attainment
provide complementary measures of performance.

The selected champions remain fixed throughout benchmarking.
Section~\ref{sec:independent_benchmark_method} describes this separation
from synthesis.

Each benchmark run is specified by the algorithm, BBOB function and
instance, dimension, evaluation noise level, objective-evaluation budget, and
random seed. The evaluation budget is $B_{\mathrm{eval}}=10{,}000D$:
20,000, 30,000, 50,000 and 100,000 instrumented objective evaluations for
$D=2,3,5,10$, respectively. Each condition targets $N_{\mathrm{runs}}=20$
trials, using instance $r$ and optimizer/noise seed $42+r$ for $r=1,\ldots,20$.
Dimension-proportional budgeting is described in the COCO experimental
procedure \cite{Hansen2016Procedure}. This study selects the multiplier
10,000, holding the permitted evaluations per dimension constant. Solvers
at the same dimension receive the same objective-evaluation allowance. It does not give different dimensions the same
absolute number of evaluations, nor guarantee comparable problem difficulty
across functions. The independent benchmark budget is also deliberately
smaller than the $10^6$-evaluation synthesis budget: synthesis selects candidate programs, while benchmarking assesses the
selected programs across instances under a smaller search allowance. The resulting
curves should therefore be interpreted at the benchmark's stated budget,
not as asymptotic performance or a full COCO restart experiment.
The generated champions have a 30-second execution timeout; the classical
baseline runners are called directly without an equivalent timeout wrapper.
This difference in execution constraints is considered when interpreting
comparisons. Solvers share the instance/seed schedule,
but consume random numbers according to their own search logic. The reference optimizers and their
implementation or parameter settings are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:reference_optimizers}.

The budget counts actual evaluations of the instrumented IOH objective,
including repeated evaluations of the same point. Once the budget is
exhausted, the wrapper returns the last observed value with a warning
instead of evaluating a new point. Consequently, the number of Python
objective-interface invocations can exceed $B_{\mathrm{eval}}$, even though
the recorded IOH evaluation count cannot. The initial search evaluation
consumes budget; the separate post-run clean assessment does not.
The framework does not restart a solver to spend unused budget. A solver
may stop early according to its own stopping rules or fail during execution;
20 instance trials must not be interpreted as 20 independent restarts on
one instance. Elapsed time is recorded separately, although timing boundaries
differ between adapters, so it is not used as the primary efficiency measure.

\subsection{Performance Measures}
\label{subsec:experiment_performance_measures}

The clean returned-point gap, convergence measure, and target-attainment
ECDF are defined in Section~\ref{subsec:benchmark_performance_measures}.
The primary success threshold is $\Delta f\leq10^{-8}$. The secondary
threshold $10^{-2}$ is also reported in target-quality diagnostics.
Each complete benchmark condition contributes 20 instance trials, and the
summary tables weight complete conditions equally, as defined in
Equation~\eqref{eq:benchmark_return_reliability}.

The IOH query histories supply the clean values in
Equation~\eqref{eq:benchmark_query_convergence}. The query histories and
terminal returned-point measurements are retained separately in the
benchmark records. Query points
are clean-scored before observational noise is added. The separate
returned-point evaluation is unlogged and does not consume the search
budget. For display, irregular trace entries are aligned by carrying the
last recorded value forward on a 300-point logarithmic grid from 1 to
$10^6$. Values shown after a trial's last actual evaluation, including
values beyond $B_{\mathrm{eval}}$, represent this display convention and
cannot create a new target attainment.

The native implicit and explicit profile figures use 51 logarithmically
spaced target gaps derived from observed positive gaps separately for each
noise level. If $\tau_{\min,\sigma}$ and $\tau_{\max,\sigma}$ denote the
positive endpoints selected for a profile export, its logarithmic grid is
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{k,\sigma}
    =10^{\log_{10}\tau_{\min,\sigma}
    +\frac{k}{50}\left(\log_{10}\tau_{\max,\sigma}
    -\log_{10}\tau_{\min,\sigma}\right)},
    \qquad k=0,\ldots,50.
\label{eq:benchmark_adaptive_targets}
\end{equation}
The endpoint values are determined by the observed gaps in the corresponding
export, not by a fixed COCO target schedule. Because the target sets change,
these exploratory ECDFs are
not a fixed-target comparison across noise levels. The frozen-champion
noise-robustness figure instead compares the returned-point success rate at
the same $10^{-8}$ target across noise levels; it is not an ECDF. The primary
tables likewise use the returned-point rate at $10^{-8}$, rather than an
adaptive-target ECDF ordinate or its endpoint.

\subsection{Uncertainty and Comparisons}
\label{subsec:experiment_uncertainty}

Each condition's returned-point success rate has a 95\% Wilson interval.
Aggregate table intervals use 1,000 condition-cluster bootstrap resamples,
seed 20260923, and percentile 95\% limits; a condition and all its 20
trials are resampled together. Incomplete or stale conditions are excluded,
not treated as failures; confirmed execution failures remain failures.
Runtime exceptions and timeouts have infinite terminal error and cannot
meet either success target. The current trial handler does not retain
separate timeout and runtime-exception categories in terminal provenance.
Returned-point reliability therefore includes failure under the implemented
execution constraints as well as search performance.

The available statistical engine implements condition-wise Kruskal--Wallis
omnibus tests, two-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ comparisons, Vargha--Delaney
$A_{12}$ for minimization, and Benjamini--Hochberg FDR correction at
$\alpha=0.05$ over valid pairwise tests in one invocation.
These are unpaired tests despite the shared instance/seed schedule.
The available results include descriptive contrasts and bootstrap intervals;
numerical pairwise and omnibus test reports are unavailable. Consequently,
no significance claim is based on these unreported tests. The recorded
current IOH and LLaMEA installations are 0.3.22 and 1.2.0, respectively;
these versions were not persisted for each historical run.

\section{Benchmark Results}
\label{sec:benchmark_results}
%==============================================================================

The native comparisons evaluate champions on the function, dimension, and
noise level used during synthesis. The robustness comparison varies observation noise for a fixed
clean-origin champion. The full native condition table is retained as a
machine-readable report, alongside terminal trial records and clean query
traces.
The export contains 1,920 native condition rows, of which 1,890 have 20
completed trials. One otherwise complete row has a stale code hash:
DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite-Instruct with Explicit-Noisy Vectorization on $f_{11}$ at $D=3$ and
$\sigma=0.05$.
After checking exported champions against code and provenance, 1,889 complete
current conditions (37,780 trials) enter the descriptive summaries.
No raw-gap mean is used for the headline comparison: fixed-target success
probabilities are averaged equally across eligible conditions.
The exploratory profiles display each function separately, without an
overall raw-gap average. Function-specific scales must be considered when
interpreting convergence; raw gaps are not used for cross-function ranking.
The relation between target attainment and returned-point success is defined
in Section~\ref{subsec:benchmark_performance_measures}, particularly
Equations~\eqref{eq:benchmark_return_reliability}--\eqref{eq:benchmark_target_ecdf}.

\subsection{Generated-Program Case Studies}
\label{subsec:generated_program_cases}

Aggregate success rates do not show how a generated program makes search
decisions or why a particular instance fails. The following two selected
champions illustrate distinct behaviors; they have different benchmark
conditions and are not a controlled head-to-head comparison. Their source
code, champion identities, IOH query histories, and corresponding terminal
trial records were checked for consistency. The complete selected programs
are reproduced with line numbers in Appendix~\ref{app:generated_programs};
the excerpts below identify the decisions examined in this section.

\subsubsection{Successful Clean Search}

A DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite-Instruct Explicit-Clean Baseline champion for $f_1$ at $D=2$ is a generated
particle-swarm optimizer (experiment 2063, iteration 2). It maintains
personal-best positions and updates them when a new observation improves
the corresponding value:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=championprogram,firstnumber=41,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
improvement_mask = new_values < personal_best_values
personal_best_positions[improvement_mask] = particles[improvement_mask].copy()
personal_best_values[improvement_mask] = new_values[improvement_mask]
\end{lstlisting}
Its 20 benchmark instances all meet the returned-point target
$\Delta f\leq10^{-8}$ within the $D=2$ budget of 20,000 evaluations.
This establishes success for this specified condition, not superiority
across the whole BBOB subset. The comparison in line 41 of
Listing~\ref{lst:generated_pso_complete} accepts an improvement by comparing
two objective values directly. Adding the same constant to both values
preserves their ordering. Similarly, the global incumbent is selected by
an \texttt{argmin}, which is unchanged by a common additive offset. This
avoids the sign-sensitive parent-selection rule found in the next example.
It does not establish that every implementation detail is ideal: complete
particle batches can make the optimizer request additional calls near the
budget boundary, where the benchmark wrapper enforces the actual evaluation
limit. The evidence here concerns its observed returned-point performance
under the stated clean, two-dimensional condition.

\subsubsection{Offset-Sensitive Selection}

In contrast, the DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite-Instruct Implicit Baseline champion for noisy $f_1$ at
$D=5$ and $\sigma=0.05$ is \texttt{ImprovedSES} (experiment 2139,
iteration 9). Its recorded synthesis gap is approximately
$1.294\times10^{-8}$ on instance 1, whose optimum is $79.48$.
Selection on this instance therefore identified a near-optimal candidate
without testing whether its selection rule remained valid for other
objective offsets. Its parent filter, in lines 32--33 of
Listing~\ref{lst:generated_ses_complete}, includes the following lines:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=championprogram,firstnumber=32,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
good_candidates_index = fitnesses[idx] < self.best_y * 1.1
parents = parents[good_candidates_index]
\end{lstlisting}
Of its 20 benchmark trials, nine return finite clean errors and eleven
fail after 150 actual objective evaluations; the median terminal error
is therefore infinite. All eleven failed trials have negative instance
optimum values, whereas the nine finite-return trials have positive
optimum values. Finite return is distinct from solving the problem:
none of the 20 trials meets the primary $10^{-8}$ target, and six meet
the secondary $10^{-2}$ target. The finite-return rate is $45\%$, while
the primary and secondary success rates are $0\%$ and $30\%$.
Table~\ref{tab:ses_instance_diagnostics} contrasts representative recorded
trials. Failed trials still have finite best-queried clean gaps in the IOH
history because the program evaluated points before failing to return.

The rule is sensitive to the absolute objective offset. If $s$ is the
stored best observation, adding a constant $c$ to the objective changes
the acceptance condition as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde f(x)+c < 1.1(s+c)
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    \widetilde f(x)<1.1s+0.1c.
    \label{eq:ses_offset_sensitivity}
\end{equation}
The same shift leaves clean objective gaps and the heteroscedastic noise
scale unchanged when $f^*$ is shifted with the objective. Nevertheless,
it changes which parents this program accepts. In particular, multiplying
a negative incumbent by $1.1$ makes the cutoff more negative, rather than
allowing a candidate to be slightly worse than that incumbent.
For positive incumbents the cutoff is more permissive, and its allowance
depends on the objective offset as well as the quality of the solution.
Thus a change in instance optimum is a valid benchmark condition that
exposes a weakness in the program's selection rule.

The full source explains the observed 150-evaluation failure. At $D=5$,
the initial population contains 50 points. The first pass evaluates those
50 points and then evaluates 50 parents and mutants. The initial
infinite incumbent allows all 25 selected parents to survive. After
100 objective calls, the program has a finite incumbent. The next pass
re-evaluates the 50-point population; if the parent filter now rejects
every candidate, the new population is empty. Line 41 then calls
\texttt{np.argmin} on an empty array before reaching the return statement,
despite the earlier stored best point.

An additional diagnostic executed the unchanged source with the recorded
instance and seed schedule, stopping surviving searches after at most 200
objective calls. It reproduced the empty-population \texttt{ValueError}
at 150 calls on all eleven previously failed instances. For instance 3,
the stored best observation after the first pass was approximately
$-241.626$, giving a cutoff of $-265.789$ and zero surviving parents.
For instance 1, the corresponding observation was $87.588$, its cutoff
was $96.347$, and five parents survived. The archived terminal records
do not contain these exception details; these local-state values and the
exception were obtained by the additional diagnostic, rather than inferred
from the median alone.

To isolate the role of the offset, the same diagnostic added $326.59$ to
the observations on instance 3, retaining the initial population and
optimizer/noise seeds. The first 150 queried coordinates and underlying
noise observations were identical; only the returned scalar offset was
changed before selection. Its shifted optimum became $79.48$, and the search continued
to the 200-call diagnostic limit. Conversely, subtracting $326.59$ from
instance 1 made its optimum $-247.11$ and reproduced the empty-population
failure at 150 calls. These bounded checks identify offset sensitivity in
the early selection logic; they do not establish final convergence of the
shifted runs or replace the archived benchmark measurements.

\subsubsection{Budget Counting}

A second weakness appears in lines 22 and 39 of
Listing~\ref{lst:generated_ses_complete}. If $P=50$ is the fixed nominal
population size, $n$ the current population size, and $m$ the new population
size, the two batches consume $n+m$ objective calls. The internal counter
instead advances by $P+m-n$, differing by $P-2n$. It undercounts when
$n=P$ and can overcount after the population shrinks. For example, the
first 100 actual calls advance the counter by only 50; with a stable
ten-point population, 20 calls advance it by 50. The mutation schedule and
stopping condition depend on this incorrect counter, so the defect can
also alter search behavior in trials that return a finite solution.
The archived synthesis iteration records 1,999,268 objective evaluations
against its configured allowance of $10^6$, exposing historical budget
noncompliance that the low recorded synthesis gap alone does not reveal.
Independent benchmarking caps actual IOH evaluations at 50,000 for this
condition; its counts should therefore be used instead of the program's
counter when assessing effort. The much smaller independent search allowance
also means that the synthesis gap and the finite benchmark gaps cannot be
interpreted as a contrast of instance effects alone.

The eleven early failures consume only $0.3\%$ of the benchmark budget.
Their cause is the reproduced empty-population error, rather than budget
exhaustion. Observation noise can change candidate ordering, but the
offset-dependent comparison and absence of an empty-population guard
explain this particular failure pattern. More evaluations do not repair
that rule. Conversely, the nine finite-return trials still require
separate quality assessment: their completion does not imply that the
optimum was found. This distinction motivates reporting finite returns,
target successes, and failures together with the terminal median.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:experiment_discussion}
%==============================================================================

The results show heterogeneous behavior rather than uniform improvement
with model size or additional guidance. The common-strategy implicit comparison
does not favor the largest Qwen model, while its Explicit-Clean strategy
ablations include stronger solved rates. Explicit native noisy rates are
generally lower than clean-origin rates, but those contrasts combine changes
in synthesis and evaluation noise. Frozen-champion robustness is the more
appropriate design for isolating an evaluation-noise change.

The heteroscedastic model makes observations at the exact optimum noiseless,
but it does not guarantee that an optimizer can locate that point. Writing
$\Delta(x)=f_{\mathrm{clean}}(x)-f^*$ and $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, an observed
gap at $x$ has the form
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde\Delta(x)=\Delta(x)+\sigma|\Delta(x)|Z
    =\Delta(x)(1+\sigma Z), \qquad \Delta(x)\geq 0.
    \label{eq:experiment_relative_noise}
\end{equation}
Its absolute standard deviation tends to zero as $x$ approaches an optimum,
but its standard deviation relative to a nonzero gap remains $\sigma$.
Consequently, two nearby candidates can still be misranked when their
clean improvement is small compared with their observational uncertainty.
Repeated selection on noisy values can retain a favorable observation
instead of the point with the smallest clean gap. The optimizer must also
reach the low-noise vicinity through the wider search landscape; more
dimensions, conditioning, or multiple basins can make this harder within
the available budget. Thus the optimum itself is unperturbed while its
surrounding observations, apparent contours, and the decisions based on
them may be perturbed. Figures~\ref{fig:f8_noise_distortion} and
\ref{fig:f21_noise_distortion} visualize this mechanism, but cannot
establish that noise caused every poor outcome.

The benchmark separates these interpretations where possible. Clean
query-trace convergence and target-attainment ECDFs measure points found
during search; terminal reliability measures the returned point after an
independent clean assessment. A solver can query a good point yet return
another one. An infinite terminal gap denotes a failed or non-finite
result under the execution contract, not a numerical distance from the
optimum. For example, the \texttt{ImprovedSES} case in
Section~\ref{subsec:generated_program_cases} has nine finite returns even
though its median gap is infinite. Its sign-sensitive code illustrates
why instance-dependent failures should not automatically be attributed
to stochastic observation noise. The frozen-champion comparison better
isolates a change in evaluation noise because it keeps the selected code,
function and dimension fixed.

The reliability estimates describe selected programs across benchmark
instances. Estimating how often an LLM independently discovers a successful
program would require controlled repeated synthesis runs. Historical synthesis
repeat counts are unbalanced and seeds are unrecorded. Incomplete conditions
and the stale-hash exclusion limit coverage; descriptive rankings may change
after those conditions are completed. No statistical-significance claim
is made without exported tests. The illustrative profile figures show only
Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct at $D=2$; the condition tables cover all tested dimensions and models.

The 30-second timeout on generated champions, without an equivalent
baseline timeout, limits a purely evaluation-count-based comparison.
Timeout failures cannot be distinguished from other execution exceptions
in the current terminal export. The study therefore measures reliability
under the implemented execution constraints, rather than an unrestricted
COCO comparison.
