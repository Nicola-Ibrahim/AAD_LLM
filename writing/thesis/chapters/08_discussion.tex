The experimental results distinguish the quality of points found during
search from the quality of the solution returned at termination. This
distinction helps interpret performance under noisy observations, where
an optimizer may not recognize its most accurate queried point.
The discussion considers these outcomes alongside the behavior of the
generated programs and the limits of the experimental design.

\section{Model and Prompt Comparisons}
\label{sec:discussion_model_prompts}

Performance depends on the model, prompt strategy, and optimization
environment. The aggregate comparisons provide no consistent ordering
in which a larger model or additional prompt guidance always produces
a more successful optimizer.

Native clean and noisy comparisons change both the synthesis environment
and the evaluation environment. They therefore do not isolate the effect
of evaluation noise on an individual optimizer. The frozen-champion
comparison addresses that question by preserving the selected program,
function, and dimension while changing the observation noise.

\section{Interpreting Performance under Noise}
\label{sec:discussion_noise}

The heteroscedastic model makes observations at the exact optimum noiseless,
but it does not guarantee that an optimizer can locate that point. For a feasible point $x$, let
$\Delta(x)=\max\{0,f_{\mathrm{clean}}(x)-f^*\}$ be its clean optimality
gap. For exact objective values, $f_{\mathrm{clean}}(x)-f^*\geq0$. With
$Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, the noisy observation relative to the optimum is
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde\Delta(x)=\Delta(x)+\sigma|\Delta(x)|Z
    =\Delta(x)(1+\sigma Z), \qquad \Delta(x)\geq 0.
    \label{eq:experiment_relative_noise}
\end{equation}
Unlike the clean gap, this noisy difference can be negative.
Its absolute standard deviation tends to zero as $x$ approaches an optimum,
but its standard deviation relative to a nonzero gap remains $\sigma$.
Consequently, two nearby candidates can still be misranked when their
clean improvement is small compared with their observational uncertainty.
Repeated selection on noisy values can retain a favorable observation
instead of the point with the smallest clean gap. The optimizer must also
reach the low-noise vicinity through the wider search landscape; more
dimensions, conditioning, or multiple basins can make this harder within
the available budget. Thus the optimum itself is unperturbed while its
surrounding observations, apparent contours, and the decisions based on
them may be perturbed. Figures~\ref{fig:f8_noise_distortion} and
\ref{fig:f21_noise_distortion} visualize this mechanism, but cannot
establish that noise caused every poor outcome.

The generated-program case study in
Section~\ref{subsec:generated_program_cases} shows why poor outcomes should not
be attributed to observation noise alone. Eleven instances fail because
an offset-sensitive selection rule produces an empty population.
Although those trials evaluate points before failing, they return no
valid solution. Conversely, the nine finite returns do not meet the
primary accuracy target. Search progress, valid completion, and
returned-solution success therefore describe different aspects of
performance.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion_limitations}

The reliability estimates describe selected programs across benchmark
instances. Estimating how often an LLM independently discovers a successful
program would require controlled repeated synthesis runs. Historical synthesis
repeat counts are unbalanced and seeds are unrecorded. The excluded
conditions also limit coverage, and descriptive rankings may change
when further conditions become available. The illustrative profile figures show only
Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct at $D=2$; the condition tables cover all tested dimensions and models.

The 30-second timeout on generated champions, without an equivalent
baseline timeout, limits a purely evaluation-count-based comparison.
The reported reliability therefore includes the effect of these
execution constraints as well as optimization performance.
The synthesis case study also identifies a candidate that exceeded the
configured $10^6$-evaluation allowance. Its near-optimal synthesis result
therefore cannot be interpreted as performance achieved within that
allowance. Independent benchmarking enforces its own evaluation budget,
but the earlier violation limits interpretation of the synthesis process.

The conclusions are limited to the tested LLM configurations, prompt
variants, benchmark functions and instances, dimensions, noise model,
budgets, and repetitions. They do not establish that the same performance
will hold for other models, prompts, or optimization problems.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion_conclusion}

The study combines locally hosted LLMs, optimizer synthesis, and a
BBOB evaluation environment with controlled observation noise. Separate
clean assessments allow search progress and returned-solution quality
to be examined even when the optimizer receives noisy values.

The results demonstrate successful optimization for some selected
programs and conditions, alongside failures that become visible when
the programs are evaluated across problem instances. The case studies
show why a low synthesis gap alone is insufficient evidence of a
reliable optimizer. Assessment across instances must consider accuracy,
valid completion, and compliance with the evaluation allowance.
