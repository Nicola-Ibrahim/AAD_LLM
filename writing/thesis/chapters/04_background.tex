\chapter{Background}
\label{chap:background}

Continuous black-box optimization considers problems in which an algorithm
must make decisions from objective-function evaluations without access to the
analytical structure of the objective. In practical applications, these
evaluations may also be affected by uncertainty, so that the observed fitness
does not always represent the underlying objective exactly. This chapter
introduces the concepts needed to study this setting and reviews previous work
on automated algorithm design using large language models (LLMs).

The chapter first introduces continuous black-box optimization, the BBOB
benchmark suite, and established derivative-free methods. It then examines
noisy fitness evaluation and its consequences for optimization. The discussion
moves to Automated Algorithm Design (AAD), program synthesis, and LLM-based
evolutionary approaches. Particular attention is given to LLaMEA's elitist
$(1+1)$ variant, in which an LLM proposes executable optimization algorithms.
The chapter concludes by considering how uncertain performance estimates can
affect selection during algorithm synthesis.

\section{Continuous Optimization}
\label{sec:continuous_optimization}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Continuous black-box optimization seeks the global minimum of an unknown
real-valued objective function:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bbob_min}
    \min_{x \in \mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^D} f(x),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}$ denotes a bounded $D$-dimensional continuous search space.
The BBOB functions are defined on $\mathbb{R}^D$, with $[-5,5]^D$ as their
standard search domain of interest \cite{BBOB2009}. In this thesis, the
search bounds are enforced during candidate evaluations. The analytical
gradients $\nabla f(x)$ and the internal formulation of the objective are not
available to the optimizer. Instead, the algorithm learns about the search
landscape from objective-function evaluations.

This setting occurs in scientific and engineering problems where objective
values are obtained through measurements, simulations, or computational
procedures rather than a tractable analytical expression. In the finite-search
setting considered by Wolpert and Macready, the No Free Lunch theorem
establishes equal average performance across algorithms when averaged
uniformly over all objective functions under the theorem's assumptions \cite{Wolpert1997}. The
characteristics of the optimization problem therefore matter when choosing
or designing an optimization method.

Several properties can make a continuous black-box problem difficult. These
include separability, conditioning, modality, and interactions between
decision variables. In a separable function, the variables can be optimized
independently, whereas non-separable functions contain dependencies between
variables. Ill-conditioned functions have directions with substantially
different sensitivities, which can make progress difficult for methods that do
not adapt their search geometry. Multimodal functions contain multiple local
optima and therefore require the optimizer to balance exploration of the
search space with exploitation of promising regions.

Because optimization performance depends on these characteristics,
standardized benchmark suites are useful for comparing algorithms under
reproducible conditions. The Black-Box Optimization Benchmark (BBOB) suite
provides a collection of continuous test functions with different landscape
structures \cite{BBOB2009}. The suite is integrated into experimental
frameworks such as \texttt{IOHexperimenter} \cite{deNobel2024} and COCO
\cite{Hansen2021COCO}. The known optima of the benchmark functions also make
it possible to measure the progress of an optimizer relative to a known
reference solution.

to their structural characteristics. Table~\ref{tab:bbob_groups} summarizes
these groups.

BBOB performance is commonly presented using empirical cumulative
distribution functions (ECDFs). An ECDF summarizes the fraction of run--target
pairs that attain their specified precision within a given number of function
evaluations, aggregated over the selected benchmark problems and instances
\cite{Hansen2016Performance}. A commonly used target in the noiseless
benchmark setting is an optimality gap of
\begin{equation}
    \Delta f = f(x)-f(x^*) \leq 10^{-8}.
\end{equation}
Such evaluations consider both solution quality and the number of objective
evaluations required to reach a given level of precision.

A number of derivative-free and evolutionary methods are commonly used for
continuous black-box optimization. Nelder--Mead maintains a simplex of
candidate points and updates it through operations such as reflection,
expansion, contraction, and shrinkage \cite{Nelder1965}. Since it does not
require derivatives, it can be used when only objective-function evaluations
are available.

Differential Evolution (DE) is a population-based method that generates new
candidate vectors using differences between existing population members
\cite{Storn1997}. Mutation and crossover allow the method to explore the
continuous search space while making use of information contained in the
current population. Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) uses a population of
particles whose movement is influenced by their own best-known positions
and information about successful positions found by other particles
\cite{Kennedy1995}.

Evolution Strategies form another important class of continuous optimization
methods. They use stochastic variation and selection to update candidate
solutions rather than relying on a fixed geometric search structure. The
Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) is particularly
important because it adapts the covariance matrix of its search distribution
according to the observed performance of candidate solutions
\cite{Hansen2001}. This allows the search distribution to adapt to the
geometry of the objective landscape and makes CMA-ES effective on problems
with ill-conditioning and non-separability. It has consequently become a
common reference method for continuous black-box optimization.

The design of these algorithms has traditionally required substantial human
expertise. Modular frameworks allow designers to explore predefined choices
systematically. Modular CMA-ES combines options for its sampling, covariance,
and restart behavior \cite{vanRijn2016,deNobel2021}. Modular Differential
Evolution offers choices such as mutation, crossover, and selection strategies
\cite{Vermetten2023ModularDE}. Automated configuration can compare combinations
of these modules, although the available design choices depend on the module
library supplied by the researchers.

Measurement-based applications help motivate noisy objective evaluations.
Plett's work on battery state-of-charge estimation addresses measurement and
model uncertainty in battery management \cite{Plett2004}. Structural model
updating likewise relies on measured vibration data, which can contain
measurement errors \cite{Mottershead1993,Marwala2010}. These applications
motivate the discussion of uncertainty below; the benchmark noise
distributions are separate, explicitly defined models.

\section{Noisy Optimization}
\label{sec:noisy_optimization}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

In practical optimization, repeated evaluations of the same candidate
solution do not necessarily return the same fitness value. Stochasticity can
come from measurement errors, environmental effects, simulation randomness,
or other sources of uncertainty. The optimizer therefore does not always
observe the underlying objective directly.

Jin and Branke distinguish four categories of uncertainty: noisy fitness
evaluations, robustness to perturbations of design variables or environmental
parameters, fitness approximation, and time-varying objectives
\cite{Jin2005}. The focus here is on noise in fitness evaluations, where the
candidate solution itself is unchanged but the objective value returned to
the optimizer is perturbed.

A general additive representation is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:noisy_evaluation_bg}
    f_{\text{noisy}}(x) = f_{\text{clean}}(x) + z,
\end{equation}
where $f_{\text{clean}}(x)$ is the underlying objective and $z$ is a random
disturbance. The observed value $f_{\text{noisy}}(x)$ is therefore a random
variable, even when the same coordinate vector $x$ is evaluated repeatedly.

Different probability distributions can be used to model fitness noise.
The following are simple additive examples. The named noisy BBOB variants
also include Uniform, Cauchy, and Gaussian disturbances, but their definitions
are function-dependent and need not equal these additive examples
\cite{Hansen2009Noisy}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Additive Uniform Noise:}
    The disturbance is bounded within a fixed interval:
    \begin{equation}
        z \sim \mathcal{U}(-a,a),
        \qquad a>0.
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Additive Cauchy Noise:}
    The Cauchy distribution introduces heavy-tailed deviations:
    \begin{equation}
        p(z)=
        \frac{1}{\pi\gamma\left[1+(z/\gamma)^2\right]},
        \qquad \gamma>0.
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Additive Gaussian Noise:}
    The disturbance is normally distributed:
    \begin{equation}
        z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2).
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

Uniform noise limits the disturbance to a fixed range, whereas Cauchy noise
can produce occasional large deviations because of its heavy tails.
Gaussian noise gives a continuous model in which the variance can be
controlled through $\sigma$. The choice of noise model therefore affects
both the frequency and magnitude of deviations between observed and
underlying fitness.

The effect of noise becomes especially important when an algorithm makes
decisions based on relative fitness. Consider two candidate solutions for
which
\begin{equation}
    f_{\text{clean}}(x_1) < f_{\text{clean}}(x_2).
\end{equation}
According to the clean objective, $x_1$ is the better solution. A particular
noise realization can nevertheless result in
\begin{equation}
    f_{\text{noisy}}(x_1) > f_{\text{noisy}}(x_2).
\end{equation}
The observed ranking is then reversed. An optimizer that relies on this
ranking may consequently make a selection decision that differs from the one
it would make using the clean objective.

The consequences depend on the optimization method. Direct-search and
evolution-strategy methods can respond differently to noisy evaluations
\cite{ArnoldB03,Arnold2003}. Small objective differences can become difficult
to distinguish from evaluation noise, affecting decisions about the local
search direction or step size. As a search radius decreases, objective
differences may become comparable to the noise magnitude. Decisions based
on these observations can then reduce the search radius even when the
current solution remains far from the optimum.

Arnold and Beyer analyzed how maintaining multiple candidate solutions can
improve the local performance of Evolution Strategies under noise
\cite{Arnold2003}. The benefits of population-based search depend on the
noise model and the optimization setting.

CMA-ES provides a more direct example of the effect of noisy ranking. Its
population update depends on the ordering of candidate solutions according
to their measured fitness \cite{Hansen2001}. The generation mean can be
written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cma_mean_update_bg}
    m^{(g+1)} =
    m^{(g)} +
    \sum_{i=1}^{\mu}
    w_i
    \left(
        x_{i:\lambda}-m^{(g)}
    \right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    w_1 \geq w_2 \geq \dots \geq w_\mu > 0,
    \qquad
    \sum_{i=1}^{\mu}w_i=1
\end{equation}

are the normalized weights associated with candidates
ordered according to their observed fitness,
\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{obs}}(x_{1:\lambda})
    \leq \dots \leq
    f_{\mathrm{obs}}(x_{\lambda:\lambda}).
\end{equation}

When the fitness evaluations are noisy, this ranking can be corrupted. A
candidate with a worse clean objective may receive a better noisy evaluation
and consequently a larger selection weight. These rank inversions can affect
both cumulative step-size adaptation and covariance-matrix learning
\cite{Jin2005,Hansen2009}. Noise can therefore influence not only which
candidate is selected, but also the search distribution used in later
generations.

Repeated evaluations can improve a fitness estimate when evaluations are
independent and their errors have finite variance. For unbiased observations
with variance $\sigma^2$, the mean of $N$ repetitions has variance
$\sigma^2/N$ \cite{Jin2005,Qian2020}. The additional evaluations consume
the same budget that could otherwise test new candidates. A Cauchy
distribution has no finite variance, so this variance formula does not apply
to the Cauchy example above.

Finite-difference gradients have a related difficulty. A forward difference
uses two objective observations divided by a step $h$. With independent
additive errors of variance $\sigma^2$, its noise contribution has variance
$2\sigma^2/h^2$. Smaller steps can therefore amplify observation noise,
even as they reduce deterministic finite-difference error \cite{Spall2003}.

Overall, fitness noise does not only influence the final solution produced by
an optimizer. It can change the information available to the search process
at every iteration. An optimization algorithm designed and evaluated under
deterministic assumptions may therefore behave differently once its objective
evaluations become stochastic.

\section{Automated Algorithm Design}
\label{sec:automated_algorithm_design}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The difficulty of manually designing and configuring optimization algorithms
has motivated research into Automated Algorithm Design (AAD). Earlier
methods include genetic programming for generating procedures and
hyper-heuristics for selecting or generating heuristics \cite{Burke2013}.
Instead of manually specifying every algorithmic decision, these methods
search over a predefined representation of possible solutions.

Modular algorithm design developed this idea further by decomposing existing
optimization algorithms into interchangeable components
\cite{vanRijn2016,deNobel2021}. This allows a large number of algorithm
configurations to be explored without requiring each combination to be
implemented manually. At the same time, the available components and the
representation of the algorithm still constrain what can be generated.

Program synthesis provides a broader search space because the system can
generate executable program structures rather than only selecting from a
fixed set of components. The development of large language models has made
this approach increasingly practical, since LLMs can generate and modify
source code from natural-language instructions.

Romera-Paredes et al.\ introduced FunSearch, which combines a language model
with an automated evaluation process to search program space
\cite{Romera2023}. The framework constrains the LLM to generate particular
program components within a larger structure and uses evaluation results to
identify promising programs. This work showed that LLM-generated program
variations can discover algorithmic solutions that perform competitively with
human-designed approaches.

Other work has combined LLM generation with evolutionary search in different
ways. EvoLLM uses a language model to propose an update to the mean of a search
distribution from discretized population data; candidate points are then
sampled from the updated distribution \cite{Lange2024}. The generated
output is a numerical update rather than a complete optimizer program.

Other approaches operate directly on heuristic code. Evolution of
Heuristics (EoH) uses LLMs to generate and evolve heuristic ideas
and their executable implementations \cite{Liu2024}. Algorithm
Evolution using LLMs (AEL) combines LLM-based generation with
evolutionary mechanisms to construct heuristic algorithms
\cite{AEL2024}. Reflective Evolution (ReEvo) combines evolutionary
search with LLM-generated reflections to guide heuristic design
\cite{ReEvo2024}. These approaches use LLMs within iterative search
processes rather than only as one-shot code generators.

Unlike EvoLLM, these systems evolve heuristic implementations rather than
numerical updates. Their evaluation results influence subsequent generations,
although feedback is incorporated differently across methods.

The literature also differs in the information provided to the LLM before
generation. AutoEP uses online exploratory landscape analysis to inform
LLM-driven hyperparameter control during combinatorial optimization
\cite{AutoEP2026}. It addresses landscape-informed control rather than the
effects of noisy BBOB evaluations on algorithm synthesis.

Other approaches make use of execution feedback. Reflexion incorporates
feedback and self-reflections into subsequent trials through an episodic
memory, without updating the model's weights \cite{Shinn2023}.
Chen et al.\ \cite{Chen2023} found that execution feedback can improve Python function
generation accuracy while reducing the number of generation samples required.
For executable algorithm synthesis, this type of feedback can include
numerical performance as well as syntax errors, runtime exceptions, and
other execution information.

Prompt optimization provides another way to shape the generation process.
EvoPrompt treats prompts themselves as objects of evolutionary optimization
\cite{Guo2023}. Promptomatix develops automated prompt optimization from task
descriptions \cite{Murthy2025}. These approaches show that prompts can become
part of the search mechanism rather than remaining fixed instructions. Their
reported evaluations do not directly address the synthesis of continuous
optimizers under stochastic fitness.

Structural information can also be used when generating source code.
LLaMEA-SAGE extends the LLaMEA approach with Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) and
Explainable AI (XAI) information to characterize generated algorithms
\cite{LLaMEASAGE2026}. Van Stein et al.\ \cite{vanStein2025} outline a research
agenda for understanding the link between generated algorithm behavior and
performance. These approaches suggest that algorithm synthesis can use
information beyond a single scalar fitness value.

The reported experiments in Table~\ref{tab:llm_aad_comparison} differ in
task and evaluation protocol. The studies provide evidence about algorithm
generation and feedback, but do not establish how generated continuous
optimizers behave when objective observations during search are noisy. This
is a more specific question than whether an LLM can produce executable code.

\section{LLaMEA and Selection Under Noise}
\label{sec:selection_under_noise}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Evolutionary algorithms iteratively apply variation, evaluation, and
selection to candidate solutions \cite{Eiben2015}. A $(1+1)$ Evolution
Strategy maintains one parent and generates one offspring per iteration.
One of the two survives based on a fitness comparison. If fitness estimates
are inaccurate, selection may retain an inferior candidate
\cite{ArnoldB03,Jin2005}. Repeated evaluations can reduce this
uncertainty at an additional evaluation cost, while other selection
mechanisms need not require extra evaluations \cite{Jin2005}.

LLaMEA applies this evolutionary principle to program space: the individuals
are executable optimization algorithms rather than numerical solutions
\cite{LLaMEA2024}. The LLM serves as a semantic variation operator,
generating new algorithm implementations from the selected program and
evaluation feedback.

Candidate algorithms in LLaMEA are represented as self-contained Python
classes following an object-oriented interface. Each candidate provides an
initialization method, \texttt{\_\_init\_\_}, which initializes parameters
and internal state, and an execution method,
\texttt{\_\_call\_\_(f)}, which uses the supplied objective function
to search within the budget specified by the evaluation setup. The paper
provides this signature in its example prompt \cite{LLaMEA2024}.

In the elitist $(1+1)$ variant, the LLM receives the initial task,
previous generation results, and the current best algorithm. Following
van Stein and B\"ack \cite{LLaMEA2024}, the feedback prompt is represented as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:llamea_context_assembly}
    \begin{aligned}
        F = \bigl(
        &S,\,
        ((\mathrm{name}(a_0), y_0), \ldots,
        (\mathrm{name}(a_t), y_t)),\\
        &(a_b, y_b, \sigma_b, e_b),\, F_0
        \bigr),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the initial task prompt, and the history
contains the names and mean performance scores $y_i$
of previously generated algorithms. The tuple
$(a_b, y_b, \sigma_b, e_b)$ represents the best-so-far
algorithm, its mean score, standard deviation, and
execution error information. Finally, $F_0$ specifies
the feedback instruction, asking the LLM to refine
or redesign the selected algorithm.

The generated response must be converted into executable source code before
evaluation. Code extraction removes conversational text surrounding the
generated program. The resulting candidate is then loaded into the execution
environment and evaluated using the optimization benchmark infrastructure.
In the original LLaMEA study, candidate algorithms are evaluated on
deterministic functions using \texttt{IOHexperimenter}
\cite{deNobel2024,LLaMEA2024}. Performance is tracked using the
objective gap $\Delta f$ introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:continuous_optimization}; the BBOB target
$\Delta f \leq 10^{-8}$ serves as a high-precision reference
\cite{Hansen2010,LLaMEA2024}.

LLaMEA uses the normalized Area Over the Convergence Curve (AOCC)
to measure performance over the entire optimization run rather
than only its final solution \cite{LLaMEA2024}. For algorithm
$a$ on benchmark function $f$, let $\mathbf{y}_{a,f}$ denote
the sequence of best-so-far, log-scaled precision values over
$B$ objective evaluations. Following the original paper,
AOCC is defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:aocc_metric_bg}
    \operatorname{AOCC}(\mathbf{y}_{a,f}) =
    \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}
    \left(
        1 -
        \frac{
            \min\{\max\{y_i,\mathrm{lb}\},\mathrm{ub}\}
            - \mathrm{lb}
        }{
            \mathrm{ub}-\mathrm{lb}
        }
    \right),
\end{equation}
where $y_i$ is the best-so-far log-scaled precision at
evaluation $i$, and $\mathrm{lb}$ and $\mathrm{ub}$ denote
the clipping bounds. The underlying precision is measured
as the difference between the best objective value observed
so far and the known optimum $f(x^*)$. Smaller gaps reached
earlier yield higher AOCC values. The original LLaMEA study
uses a raw precision range from $10^{-8}$ to $10^2$ and
applies logarithmic scaling before computing AOCC
\cite{LLaMEA2024}.

One AOCC value measures one optimizer run on one benchmark instance. To
score a generated algorithm, the original LLaMEA study aggregates AOCC over
benchmark functions, instances, and repeated optimizer runs
\cite{LLaMEA2024}. Let $\widehat{F}(\mathcal{A})$ denote the estimated mean
AOCC for algorithm $a$; higher values indicate better anytime performance.
The benchmark functions in the original experiment were noiseless, although
runs could still vary because the optimizer itself was stochastic. The known
optimum $f(x^*)$ is used by the evaluator to calculate the precision measure.

In the original LLaMEA experiment, selection in the $(1+1)$ variant can be
written as the replacement rule:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:one_plus_one_selection}
    \mathcal{A}_{\text{parent}}^{(g+1)} =
    \begin{cases}
        \mathcal{A}_{\text{child}}^{(g)}
        &
        \text{if }
        \widehat{F}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{child}}^{(g)})
        \geq
        \widehat{F}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{parent}}^{(g)}),
        \\
        \mathcal{A}_{\text{parent}}^{(g)}
        &
        \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Here $\widehat{F}$ is the original experiment's estimated aggregate AOCC,
so higher is better. The rule describes LLaMEA's elitist $(1+1)$ variant;
the paper also examines a non-elitist $(1,1)$ variant \cite{LLaMEA2024}.
In the elitist case, the retained parent becomes the starting point for the
next generation.

Execution errors and variability across repeated runs can also inform the
feedback presented to the LLM. In the original scoring formulation, a failed
evaluation receives the worst score \cite{LLaMEA2024}.

Noisy objective observations can alter the search trajectory
and returned solution of a generated optimizer. These changes
may also affect its assessed performance, depending on the
evaluation protocol. The Methodology chapter specifies how
generated algorithms are evaluated in this thesis.

\section{Discussion of Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work_discussion}
%---------------------------------------------------------------

The reviewed approaches differ in what the LLM generates and
how evaluation feedback is used. Some methods produce numerical
updates or optimize prompts and hyperparameters, while others
generate executable heuristics. Their evaluation settings also
differ, making direct comparisons between the reported results
difficult.

LLaMEA is the main reference for this thesis because it generates
executable continuous optimization algorithms and evaluates
them on BBOB benchmarks. Its original evaluation uses the
noiseless BBOB suite \cite{LLaMEA2024}. Together with the
challenges of noisy optimization discussed earlier, this
provides the basis for investigating LLM-generated heuristics
under controlled stochastic fitness evaluation. The following
chapters describe the experimental setup and comparisons with
established optimization methods.
