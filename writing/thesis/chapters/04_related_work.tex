\chapter{Background and Related Work}\label{chap:background}

To properly contextualize the operational environment of this research, it is necessary to examine the foundational concepts of continuous black-box optimization alongside the algorithmic vulnerabilities introduced by stochastic noise. Building upon these theoretical foundations, recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have driven a paradigm shift toward Automated Algorithm Design (AAD), enabling autonomous agents to synthesize complete, executable search strategies. This chapter establishes the mathematical background of continuous optimization under uncertainty, details the failure mechanics of classical heuristics in noisy fitness spaces, surveys state-of-the-art literature in LLM-driven code evolution and prompt scaffolding, and isolates the primary research gap addressed by this thesis.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Foundations of Continuous Optimization and Stochasticity}\label{sec:bg_theory}

Continuous black-box optimization focuses on identifying the global minimum of an unknown, real-valued objective function:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bbob_min}
    \min_{x \in \mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^D} f(x)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}$ represents a bounded $D$-dimensional continuous search space (typically $[-5, 5]^D$). Because the analytical gradient $\nabla f(x)$ and any internal structural equations governing the objective are completely inaccessible, point sampling queries constitute the sole mechanism for information retrieval. Due to the immense topological diversity of real-world fitness landscapes—characterized by high conditioning, non-separability, and multi-modality—the No Free Lunch theorem dictates that no single general-purpose solver can achieve optimal performance across all problem instances without specialized adaptation \cite{Burke2013}. Custom-tailoring unique algorithmic logic or tuning parameters for every distinct application represents an engineering bottleneck, as manual design is heavily labor-intensive, domain-specific, and unscalable \cite{Burke2013}.

In standard literature, algorithms designed for these unconstrained or box-constrained settings are systematically benchmarked against the Black-Box Optimization Benchmark (BBOB) suite \cite{Hansen2010}. Standardized within the IOHexperimenter \cite{deNobel2024} and COCO platforms, the BBOB testbed comprises 24 synthetic mathematical functions engineered to emulate distinct topological challenges encountered in complex physical engineering. As detailed in Table~\ref{tab:bbob_groups}, these benchmarks are categorized into five structural problem groups based on their underlying landscape properties.

Crucially, the BBOB framework standardized the use of empirical cumulative distribution functions (ECDFs) to characterize algorithm performance, measuring runtime by the number of function evaluations required to achieve a specific target precision delta ($\Delta f_t = f(x) - f(x^*) \le 10^{-8}$). Over recent decades, human engineering has yielded robust heuristics to navigate these deterministic spaces, most notably the Nelder-Mead simplex algorithm \cite{Nelder1965}, Differential Evolution (DE) \cite{Storn1997}, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) \cite{Kennedy1995}, and the Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) \cite{Hansen2001}. Empirical baselines on the noiseless BBOB testbed consistently demonstrate that CMA-ES serves as the state-of-the-art standard for continuous optimization, outperforming local search methods like NEWUOA \cite{Powell2006} by multiple orders of magnitude on highly conditioned, non-separable topologies due to its robust covariance matrix update mechanics.

To alleviate the manual trial-and-error bottleneck of inventing metaheuristics, researchers subsequently introduced modular algorithm design frameworks \cite{vanRijn2016, deNobel2021}. Modular CMA-ES \cite{vanRijn2016} and Modular DE \cite{deNobel2021} decompose classical algorithms into discrete, configurable blocks (e.g., active covariance updates, mirrored sampling, or adaptive step-size scaling). While these frameworks establish combinatorial search spaces encompassing millions of candidate configurations, they remain strictly bounded by human imagination: an automated configurator can only recombine module options explicitly programmed into the architecture by domain experts. Ultimately, modular approaches are restricted to predefined design spaces, motivating the need for generative methods capable of open-ended programmatic synthesis.

While human-engineered algorithms excel on deterministic functions, practical industrial applications—such as extracting parameters from physical hardware—are inherently stochastic. For instance, in Electric Vehicle (EV) manufacturing, engineers rely on continuous black-box optimization algorithms to extract physical parameters from battery sensor data by minimizing an empirical Error Space \cite{Plett2004, Xiong2018}. However, real-world sensors are prone to electrical interference and measurement limitations, producing stochastic telemetry. Similar challenges manifest in finite-element structural dynamics updating, where measurement noise corrupts parameter estimation \cite{Mottershead1993, Marwala2010}.

Objective evaluations in these environments are corrupted by sensor inaccuracies, which are systematically modeled in continuous optimization testbeds through distinct statistical distributions \cite{Hansen2009Noisy}. As established in the foundational taxonomy by Jin and Branke \cite{Jin2005}, stochastic uncertainty in optimization can disrupt the parameter space, the time space, the surrogate space, or the fitness space. This research focuses strictly on uncertainty acting within the fitness space, where the true time-invariant objective height $f_{\text{clean}}(x)$ is corrupted by an additive stochastic perturbation $z$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noisy_evaluation_bg}
    f_{\text{noisy}}(x) = f_{\text{clean}}(x) + z
\end{equation}
rendering the observed fitness a random variable centered around the true underlying metric. In continuous optimization testbeds, sensory disruptions are commonly simulated via three statistical distributions \cite{Hansen2009Noisy}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Additive Uniform Noise:} Models digital sensor quantization and truncation errors: $z \sim \mathcal{U}(-a, a)$.
    \item \textbf{Additive Cauchy Noise:} A heavy-tailed distribution modeling severe, intermittent hardware spikes or packet dropouts: $p(z) = (\pi \gamma [1 + (z/\gamma)^2])^{-1}$.
    \item \textbf{Additive Gaussian Noise:} Models background thermal and electronic white noise operating independently of signal height: $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$.
\end{enumerate}

The mathematical failure modes of direct search methods under noise were formalized by Arnold and Beyer \cite{Arnold2003}. Evaluating local search heuristics on noisy sphere landscapes ($f(x) + z$), their analysis established that pattern search, Nelder-Mead, and implicit filtering inevitably suffer from \textit{step-size stagnation}. When operating at fine spatial resolutions, true objective differentials $\Delta f_{\text{clean}}$ drop below the noise standard deviation $\sigma$. Misinterpreting these stochastic perturbations as local topological features, direct search heuristics prematurely contract their step sizes, exploding the noise-to-signal ratio and permanently freezing progress far from the global optimum. Conversely, their work demonstrated that multi-parent evolution strategies $(\mu/\mu, \lambda)\text{-ES}$ maintain stochastic linear convergence by leveraging global intermediate recombination to achieve \textit{genetic repair}—averaging out zero-mean noise across parents to sustain a $\mu$-fold larger mutation step length.

In population-based algorithms like CMA-ES \cite{Hansen2001}, distribution updates depend directly on sorting candidate solutions by relative fitness rank. The mean vector $m^{(g+1)}$ for generation $g+1$ is computed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cma_mean_update_bg}
    m^{(g+1)} = m^{(g)} + \sum_{i=1}^{\mu} w_i \left( x_{i:\lambda} - m^{(g)} \right)
\end{equation}
where $w_1 \ge w_2 \ge \dots \ge w_\mu > 0$ represent recombination weights assigned according to sorted performance $f(x_{1:\lambda}) \le \dots \le f(x_{\lambda:\lambda})$. When fitness evaluations are corrupted by noise $z$, the empirical rank ordering is inverted ($f_{\text{noisy}}(x_{i:\lambda}) > f_{\text{noisy}}(x_{j:\lambda})$ despite true $f_{\text{clean}}(x_i) < f_{\text{clean}}(x_j)$). This rank corruption misallocates dominant recombination weights to sub-optimal vectors, distorting both step-size adaptation $\sigma^{(g)}$ and covariance matrix accumulation $C^{(g)}$ \cite{Hansen2009Noisy, Sarafian2024}. As confirmed by Jin and Branke \cite{Jin2005}, classical human-engineered workarounds—such as static sample averaging or expanding population sizes—severely degrade optimization efficiency due to the $\sqrt{N}$ sample size penalty. Furthermore, this vulnerability is even more severe in classical gradient-based algorithms; because these systems rely entirely on computing exact analytical or finite-difference derivatives, the addition of even minor statistical noise completely corrupts gradient estimation vectors, leading to immediate algorithmic failure \cite{Sarafian2024}. Static pre-runtime hyperparameter tuning cannot resolve this trade-off, as fixed parameter selections cannot dynamically adapt to local noise variance shifts across the search space. This creates an active mandate to investigate whether autonomous logic generators can synthesize structural algorithmic alternatives rather than relying on static computational averages.

\section{Automated Algorithm Design and Literature Review}\label{sec:related_work}

To eliminate human design bottlenecks, early Automated Algorithm Design (AAD) frameworks relied on Genetic Programming \cite{Burke2013} or hyper-heuristics. However, the immense combinatorial breadth of programmatic space and the rigid syntactic boundaries of predefined search blocks frequently restricted true algorithmic discovery. This barrier was recently overcome by the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs), validated for strict mathematical discovery by Romera-Paredes et al.~\cite{Romera2023}. Their framework, FunSearch, addresses the inherent tendency of LLMs toward hallucination by pairing the model with a systematic, closed-loop evaluator. By constraining the LLM to evolve the critical logical components of a program skeleton—rather than attempting to generate raw numerical solutions—FunSearch proved that generative models can synthesize novel, verifiable algorithmic constructions that surpass human-engineered baselines without suffering from semantic hallucination.

A comprehensive survey by Zhang et al.~\cite{ReflectiveSurvey2025} formally categorizes the operational paradigms of LLM-assisted evolutionary computation into three distinct prompt operators: selection operators that instruct the language model to evaluate and rank programmatic candidates based on empirical performance logs; variation operators that serve as semantic mutation and crossover prompts where the model refactors and recombines existing code structures; and reflective operators that inject closed-loop execution tracebacks, compiler errors, and quantitative performance deltas back into the context window to drive self-correction. Following FunSearch, the literature explored alternative modalities for utilizing LLMs in optimization pipelines, which, as categorized in Table~\ref{tab:llm_aad_comparison}, bifurcate primarily into numerical coordinate samplers and programmatic code-generation hyper-heuristics.

Investigations into numerical sampling, such as the EvoLLM framework \cite{Lange2024}, established that LLMs can act directly as evolutionary operators to predict candidate coordinate vectors. However, EvoLLM lacks code interpretability, incurs heavy token footprints, and remains restricted to low-dimensional instances. Conversely, frameworks such as Evolution of Heuristics (EoH) \cite{Liu2024}, Algorithm Evolution using LLMs (AEL) \cite{Liu2023}, and Reflective Evolution (ReEvo) \cite{ReEvo2024} restrict the LLM's generative role to writing modular, reusable code blocks. Operating within programmatic search spaces fundamentally alters evolutionary dynamics: rather than adjusting continuous coordinate variables, designing new heuristics requires navigating a highly unstructured, non-convex space of discrete code logic. AEL and EoH proved that maintaining a population of heuristic programs and applying LLM-driven crossover and mutation significantly outperforms manually designed constructive heuristics on combinatorial problems like the Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP). Despite this success, these evolutionary code-generation pipelines assume a perfectly deterministic evaluation of generated algorithms, leaving their selection stability completely untested under the rank corruption of continuous stochastic noise.

In the continuous optimization domain, recent zero-shot paradigms such as AutoEP \cite{AutoEP2026} explore leveraging Exploratory Landscape Analysis (ELA) features as prompt priors to dynamically guide LLM code generation. While AutoEP demonstrates that structural landscape features accelerate heuristic convergence, its feedback mechanism relies heavily on static ELA pre-processing over clean functions. In parallel, advancements in multi-agent architectures, including Reflexion \cite{Shinn2023} and automated self-debugging frameworks, confirmed that passing text-based execution traces and compiler tracebacks back to the generative model drastically accelerates code convergence. Notably, Chen et al.~\cite{Chen2023} demonstrated that instructing a model to perform self-debugging via execution tracebacks boosts functional accuracy by up to 12\% while matching the performance of baselines requiring a $10\times$ higher sampling budget.

Beyond static system instructions and raw execution traces, recent research emphasizes the broader role of prompt scaffolding and automated prompt evolution. To address the inefficiency of manual prompt construction, frameworks such as EvoPrompt \cite{Guo2023} connect LLMs directly with Evolutionary Algorithms to optimize discrete prompt strings using crossover and mutation operators, demonstrating that prompt strings themselves act as key evolutionary variables. To mitigate the risks of unconstrained generation, frameworks such as Promptomatix \cite{Murthy2025} explicitly utilize prompt scaffolding to wrap instructions in structured, guarded templates. Their findings confirm that zero-configuration scaffolding enforces strict output structures and aligns model behavior with predefined logic constraints without parameter fine-tuning. However, while these techniques guarantee syntactic safety in standard NLP tasks, their efficacy as structural control variables for guiding code-level mutations inside stochastic continuous optimization environments remains critically unexplored.

In the domain of programmatic code generation, providing structural scaffolding—such as Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) feature extraction and Explainable AI (XAI) feedback utilized in LLaMEA-SAGE \cite{LLaMEASAGE2026}—has been proven to effectively bias LLM-driven variation operators. By augmenting reflective prompts with explicit structural code metrics and surrogate models rather than relying solely on raw numerical fitness scores, LLaMEA-SAGE significantly accelerates the discovery of high-performing continuous optimization heuristics. Extending this direction, van Stein \cite{vanStein2025} proposed a broader vision for explainable automated algorithm design, integrating LLM-driven discovery with problem-class descriptors to attribute performance gains to specific code structures. Comprehensive surveys \cite{ZhangSurvey2025} corroborate that structuring language models as high-level managers and semantic variation operators represents the definitive state-of-the-art for metaheuristic generation. However, both EvoPrompt and LLaMEA-SAGE evaluate prompt and structural scaffolding exclusively under deterministic, noise-free objective evaluations.

At the theoretical foundation of automated algorithmic discovery lies Evolutionary Computation (EC) \cite{Burke2013}. Classical Evolutionary Algorithms (EAs) navigate complex objective landscapes by iteratively executing three canonical operations: variation, fitness evaluation, and environmental selection \cite{Burke2013}. In continuous optimization spaces, multi-parent evolution strategies $(\mu/\mu_I, \lambda)\text{-ES}$ maintain demographic diversity to balance exploration and exploitation across generational cycles \cite{Arnold2003, Jin2005}. Foundational analyses by Arnold and Beyer \cite{Arnold2003} established that multi-parent recombination achieves genetic repair under stochastic uncertainty, effectively averaging out zero-mean evaluation noise to prevent premature step-size stagnation. However, while high-capacity multi-member populations $(\mu, \lambda)$ provide natural variance filtering in stochastic domains \cite{Jin2005}, they incur prohibitive computational overhead and context-window saturation during multi-turn LLM generation. 

Consequently, contemporary continuous code-generation frameworks simplify the search topology into minimal single-parent $(1+1)$ Evolution Strategies. The mathematical vulnerability of this design is rigorously exposed by Qian et al.~\cite{Qian2020}, who formally proved that standard $(1+1)$ evolutionary algorithms fail completely when objective evaluations are corrupted by noise, as simple sampling becomes ineffective against deceptive variance. Their theoretical analysis demonstrates that without population-based parent or offspring resampling strategies, the $(1+1)$ selection mechanism cannot distinguish true progression from statistical anomalies, leading to exponential running times. This theoretical failure mode directly underpins the operational behavior of single-parent LLM code discovery loops operating in stochastic continuous spaces.

Building directly upon executable code generation, self-debugging, and reflective prompt scaffolding, this thesis extends the Large Language Model Evolutionary Algorithm (LLaMEA) framework \cite{LLaMEA2024}. Engineered specifically to automate the discovery of continuous black-box optimization heuristics, LLaMEA structures an LLM within a $(1+1)$ Evolution Strategy. Under this paradigm, the language model serves as a semantic variation operator that iteratively generates complete, reusable Python classes. These candidates are executed within a validation harness via the \texttt{IOHexperimenter} benchmarking suite, with runtime error tracebacks and aggregated convergence metrics fed directly back into the context window to drive self-debugging and iterative refinement. 

To evaluate heuristic quality across continuous problem instances, foundational LLaMEA aggregates trajectory data into the normalized Area Over the Convergence Curve (AOCC) metric \cite{LLaMEA2024}. Evaluated over a computational budget of $B$ function evaluations across the standard precision range between upper bound $\text{ub} = 10^2$ and lower bound $\text{lb} = 10^{-8}$, the scalar AOCC is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aocc_metric_bg}
    \text{AOCC} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{k=1}^{B} \left( 1 - \frac{\log_{10}(\min(\max(\Delta f(x_k), 10^{-8}), 10^2)) - \log_{10}(10^{-8})}{\log_{10}(10^2) - \log_{10}(10^{-8})} \right)
\end{equation}
where $\Delta f(x_k) = f_{\text{clean}}(x_k) - f(x^*)$ denotes the uncorrupted objective distance to the global optimum at evaluation step $k$. By taking the logarithm of precision deltas clamped within $[10^{-8}, 10^2]$, this formulation normalizes anytime performance to the unit interval $[0, 1]$, where an AOCC of $1.0$ represents instantaneous convergence to target precision $\Delta f_t \le 10^{-8}$ at step $k=1$, while a score of $0.0$ indicates failure to advance beyond initial boundaries.

Empirically, foundational experiments proved that LLaMEA-generated algorithms discovered novel heuristic logic that outcompeted state-of-the-art metaheuristics, including vanilla CMA-ES and Differential Evolution, on the 5-dimensional continuous BBOB suite, while maintaining competitive generalizability when zero-shot scaled to unseen 10-dimensional and 20-dimensional instances \cite{LLaMEA2024}. Recent extensions, such as LLaMEA-BO \cite{LLaMEABO2025}, have further validated the framework's capability to synthesize Bayesian Optimization components and surrogate-assisted search logic.

However, evaluating a $(1+1)$ Evolution Strategy within a non-deterministic evaluation sandbox introduces a severe algorithmic vulnerability known as false elitism or fitness deception \cite{Jin2005, Qian2020}. Theoretical analyses have formally proven that standard $(1+1)$ evolutionary algorithms fail completely when objective evaluations are corrupted by noise, as simple point sampling becomes ineffective against deceptive variance \cite{Qian2020}. If a sub-optimal child algorithm evaluates a sequence of coordinates that receives an unusually favorable negative noise draw ($z \ll 0$), its recorded performance metric becomes artificially inflated. Consequently, this sub-optimal candidate may secure a dominant position purely due to a favorable statistical anomaly \cite{Jin2005}. Under strict elitist selection, this over-evaluated child replaces the parent and establishes a mathematically unachievable baseline score. Subsequent, structurally superior offspring are repeatedly rejected because they cannot surpass this deceptive threshold, permanently freezing the evolutionary search process.

Despite these recent advancements in Automated Algorithm Design, existing code-generation frameworks operate almost exclusively under deterministic, noiseless objective functions. As a result, the capacity of an LLM-driven evolutionary loop to discover noise-resilient search logic—and the extent to which prompt scaffolding or model choice governs this discovery—remains unexamined. This thesis directly addresses this boundary layer by investigating whether closed-loop evolutionary pressure can compel foundation models to synthesize robust continuous metaheuristics under persistent stochastic uncertainty.
